## A. Fast-takeoff and explosive-growth views

### A1. "AI 2027" (Kokotajlo, Lifland, Alexander, Larsen, Dean; April 2025) and the authors' revisions

**Thesis.** A leading lab automates coding, then AI research itself; research speed multiplies; within roughly a year of full coding automation there is superintelligence. The economy is transformed after the intelligence explosion, not before: robots and "special economic zones" scale in 2028–2029. The authors say 2027 was their modal year, not their median. The scenario forks into a "race" ending (AI takeover, humans dead by 2030) and a "slowdown" ending (aligned AI, abundance, extreme concentration of control).

**Dated predictions.**
- 2026: global AI capital spending about $1T (cost of ownership of active compute); lead lab revenue $35B; stock market up about 30%; junior software job market "in turmoil"; a 10,000-person anti-AI protest in Washington ([ai-2027.com](https://ai-2027.com/) [opened]).
- 2027: superhuman coder in a frontier lab around March 2027; hiring of new programmers nearly stops by July; 20% of Americans name AI the top national problem by October ([ai-2027.com/race](https://ai-2027.com/race) [opened]).
- 2028–2030, race ending: one million new robots a month by end-2028; an autonomous robot economy doubling in about a year; most people on a generous basic income or in make-work by 2029; extinction in 2030. Slowdown ending: lead lab valued at $10T in early 2028; both US parties promise basic income by May 2028; by 2029 poverty largely ended, billionaires become trillionaires, most government revenue comes from taxing or nationalizing AI firms ([race](https://ai-2027.com/race) [opened]; [slowdown](https://ai-2027.com/slowdown) [opened]).

**What happened.**
- The authors graded their own 2025 predictions in February 2026: quantitative progress ran at about 65% of the scenario's pace; most qualitative calls were on track; SWE-bench was behind (74.5% against a predicted 85% by mid-2025); OpenAI revenue was slightly ahead ($20B against $18B); the gap between top US labs was 0–2 months, not 3–9. A July 2026 note raised the pace estimate to about 75% ([AI Futures, 12 Feb 2026](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/grading-ai-2027s-2025-predictions) [opened]).
- Their timelines lengthened through 2025, then shortened twice in 2026. Kokotajlo's median for an "automated coder": late 2029 (January 2026) → mid-2028 (April) → November 2027 (August). Lifland's: early 2032 → mid-2030 → January 2030. Kokotajlo's median for superintelligence is March 2029; Lifland's is July 2033 ([Q1 2026 update](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/q1-2026-timelines-update) [opened]; [Q2.5 2026 update](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/q25-2026-timelines-update-uplift) [opened]).
- Revenue ran well ahead of the scenario's $35B for 2026 (see yardstick). The stock market ran behind: S&P up about 12%, not 30%. An independent forecaster notes that a government–lab clash (Pentagon versus Anthropic, February 2026) and a withheld frontier model (Anthropic's Mythos Preview, restricted to about 40 organizations) arrived earlier than the scenario placed them, and that China has been less prominent than predicted ([FutureSearch, 8 Apr 2026](https://futuresearch.ai/blog/ai-2027-one-year-later/) [opened]).

**Best critique.** The scenario's speed rests on a research-speed multiplier that nobody has observed at the assumed size. METR's best evidence is about 2x at one lab, with "research judgment" still a human bottleneck ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-rd-section-anthropic-risk-report-august-2026-review/) [opened]). Epoch's economists argue a software-only feedback loop fizzles because experiments need compute ([Erdil and Barnett](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/most-ai-value-will-come-from-broad-automation-not-from-r-d) [opened]). The economic chapters are also thin: no unemployment or GDP figures, and the smooth 2028 transition is asserted because superhuman advisers manage it.

### A2. Aschenbrenner, "Situational Awareness" (June 2024)

**Thesis.** Count the orders of magnitude: compute and algorithmic efficiency each improve about half an order of magnitude a year, plus "unhobbling" from chatbot to agent. On that trend, models that can do the work of an AI researcher are plausible by 2027, followed by an intelligence explosion and a government-led project. The industrial mobilization (power, chips, capital) is the visible leading edge.

**Dated predictions.** AGI by 2027 "strikingly plausible"; roughly 100,000x effective-compute scale-up from 2023 to 2027 ([From GPT-4 to AGI](https://situational-awareness.ai/from-gpt-4-to-agi/) [opened]). Annual AI investment about $500B in 2026, $2T in 2028, $8T in 2030; the largest training cluster about 1 GW in 2026 and 10 GW in 2028; AI at 5% of US electricity in 2026 and 20% in 2028; a big tech company at a $100B AI revenue run rate by about mid-2026 ([Racing to the Trillion-Dollar Cluster](https://situational-awareness.ai/racing-to-the-trillion-dollar-cluster/) [opened]).

**What happened.**
- Investment: ahead. Hyperscaler capex guidance alone is about $733B for 2026 ([State of AI 2026](https://www.stateof.ai/) [opened]).
- Revenue: late and in a different place. No single big tech firm reached a $100B AI run rate by mid-2026, but two labs together passed it by late summer (yardstick).
- Power: behind his 5% figure on available numbers. Data centers of all kinds use a little over 4% of US electricity ([Fortune, June 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/06/05/is-ai-a-bubble-worth-it-short-term-early-goldman-skeptic-covello-profits/) [opened]).
- A June 2026 scorecard grades three calls on track (capability, effective-compute growth, capex), one wrong (open-weight models fading; Chinese open models sit 3–6 months behind at a fraction of the price), and the AGI-by-2027 and government-project calls open ([EA Forum scorecard](https://forum-bots.effectivealtruism.org/posts/awAbNh3AnDwKjQtXR/situational-awareness-a-two-year-scorecard) [opened]; the post says it was AI-assisted, so treat it as informal).

**Best critique.** Straight lines on log plots end. Erdil points out that internet revenue also grew explosively until 2000, and that no benchmark measures "can automate remote work" directly ([Erdil, April 2025](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/the-case-for-multi-decade-ai-timelines) [opened]). Dwarkesh Patel adds that models still do not learn on the job ([Patel, June 2025](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/timelines-june-2025) [opened]).

### A3. Davidson and Forethought: intelligence explosion and "industrial explosion"

**Thesis.** Three feedback loops can each accelerate: software (AI improves AI algorithms), chips, and full-stack industry (robots build robot factories). Once AI research is automated, a software explosion is "at least decently likely". Physical output follows more slowly, because robots take longer to build than model copies take to launch, but it can still become very fast. The policy conclusion is that a century of technological change could arrive in about a decade, and institutions must prepare for it now.

**Quantitative claims.**
- AI could compress about 100 years of technical progress into 10; AI–human research parity is "fairly likely" within one or two decades ([MacAskill and Moorhouse, March 2025](https://www.forethought.org/research/preparing-for-the-intelligence-explosion) [opened]).
- After full automation of AI R&D, software efficiency could double every month or two at first; returns to software R&D are estimated at 0.5–2 with hardware fixed, so whether the loop accelerates is uncertain ([Eth and Davidson, March 2025](https://www.forethought.org/research/will-ai-r-and-d-automation-cause-a-software-intelligence-explosion) [opened]).
- Industrial explosion in three stages: AI-directed human labor (about a 10x one-off gain in physical output), autonomous robot factories (initial robot doubling time around a year, against 6 years for the last doubling of the world robot stock), then nanotechnology (doubling times of days or less) ([Davidson and Hadshar, May 2025](https://www.forethought.org/research/the-industrial-explosion) [opened]). The authors call the note rough and low-confidence.

**What happened.** None of the dated claims has matured. Supportive: METR's roughly 2x acceleration at Anthropic and the State of AI finding that Claude "led" 26% of measured model R&D work at Anthropic in August 2026, up from under 1% in February ([State of AI 2026](https://www.stateof.ai/) [opened]). Unsupportive: the physical side is nowhere near stage 2; the leading humanoid program has no robots doing useful work (yardstick).

**Best critique.** Where does demand for that much physical output come from, and what about permits, land, power and local opposition? Gallup finds 71% of Americans oppose a local AI data center, and at least 45 projects worth nearly $68B were blocked or delayed in one quarter ([State of AI 2026](https://www.stateof.ai/) [opened]). The model treats human and political friction as a caveat when it may be the binding constraint.

### A4. Epoch AI: the GATE model and the Barnett–Erdil–Besiroglu essays

**Thesis.** Standard growth theory, taken seriously, predicts explosive growth once AI substitutes for labor across most tasks, because output can then be reinvested in more "workers". The channel is broad automation of ordinary work, not a research-only singularity. The same authors hold long timelines to full automation, so they combine "explosive eventually" with "not soon".

**Quantitative claims.**
- GATE: during the automation period world output grows 2–20 times the historical 3% a year; optimal AI investment could exceed 10% of world GDP; the authors say the outputs are conditional and qualitative, not Epoch forecasts ([Announcing GATE, March 2025](https://epoch.ai/blog/announcing-gate) [opened]; [GATE documentation](https://epoch.ai/gate) [opened]).
- A follow-up finds growth above 20% a year with about 30% of tasks automated, and above 30% needing 50–70%; the default run's 23% growth in 2027 is flagged by its own authors as very unlikely ([Potlogea and Ho, June 2025](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/ai-and-explosive-growth-redux) [opened]).
- Automating all remote work alone would "highly likely" double US GDP and more likely than not raise it tenfold ([Barnett, January 2025](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/consequences-of-automating-remote-work) [opened]).
- R&D explains only about 20% of US labor-productivity growth since 1988; a software-only singularity would fizzle within an order of magnitude; job displacement will be gradual, visible and politically salient before anything explosive ([Erdil and Barnett, March 2025](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/most-ai-value-will-come-from-broad-automation-not-from-r-d) [opened]).
- Erdil's median for full automation of remote work is about 20 years, with about 30% on 10 years; he would be surprised if AI revenue doubled every year for eight years ([Erdil, April 2025](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/the-case-for-multi-decade-ai-timelines) [opened]).

**What happened.** Revenue more than tripled in the first eight months of 2026, faster than Erdil's slowing-growth central case. Their "broad, gradual, salient" picture fits the labor data better than the R&D-first picture: entry-level effects are visible, aggregate effects are not. GATE's implied optimal investment ($25T in 2025) is more than twenty-five times the roughly $900B that JPMorgan estimates the world is spending on AI in 2026 ([Trefis](https://www.trefis.com/articles/622421/what-s-happening-with-ai-stocks/2026-11-05) [opened]), which either means investors doubt the premise or the model omits what constrains them.

**Best critique.** The models assume AI is a perfect substitute for labor on a growing share of tasks and ignore robots, diffusion lags and market power; critics say that builds the conclusion into the assumptions. From the other side, takeoff researchers say GATE is too conservative because it has no software feedback loop (both critiques are reported in [Potlogea and Ho](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/ai-and-explosive-growth-redux) [opened]).

### A5. Dario Amodei: "Machines of Loving Grace" (October 2024) and "The Adolescence of Technology" (January 2026)

**Thesis.** "Powerful AI", a country of geniuses in a data center, could arrive within a few years and compress a century of biological and medical progress into five to ten years. Intelligence is not the only input, so physical experiments, data, regulation and human institutions cap the speed. The same technology threatens fast white-collar displacement and extreme wealth concentration, which he says needs progressive taxation and deliberate redistribution.

**Dated predictions.**
- Powerful AI possible as early as 2026; 50–100 years of biology in 5–10 years; a "dream scenario" of 20% annual growth in developing countries ([Machines of Loving Grace](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace) [opened]).
- Half of entry-level white-collar jobs displaced within 1–5 years of 2025; sustained 10–20% GDP growth may be possible; powerful AI plausibly 1–2 years off; AI-sector revenue could reach about $3T a year and valuations $30T, with personal fortunes in the trillions ([The Adolescence of Technology](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology) [opened]). His May 2025 remark that unemployment could reach 10–20% is widely reported but I could not open the original ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/28/ai-jobs-white-collar-unemployment-anthropic) [secondary]).
- March 2025: AI would write 90% of code within 3–6 months.

**What happened.** "Powerful AI in 2026" has not arrived by November. The 90%-of-code claim was graded false on its original timing by a year-end review ([LessWrong, "2025 in AI predictions"](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/69qnNx8S7wkSKXJFY/2025-in-ai-predictions) [opened]), though Anthropic later reported Claude writing about 80% of its merged code ([80,000 Hours](https://80000hours.org/?p=98607) [opened]). On jobs, eighteen months into the 1–5-year window, the aggregate rate is 4.1% and recent-graduate unemployment is 5.2%, while the relative decline for young workers in exposed occupations has widened to 19% (yardstick). His own company's revenue path (about 10x a year) is the strongest evidence for his capability claims.

**Best critique.** He runs a company that gains from the belief; Fortune called the 2026 essay part marketing ([Fortune, 27 Jan 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/01/27/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-essay-warning-ai-adolescence-test-humanity-risks-remedies/) [secondary]). Substantively, his own list of limiting factors (physical experiments, regulation, human adoption) is the skeptics' case, and he gives no account of how 10–20% growth gets through them.

### A6. Sam Altman's essays

**Thesis.** Intelligence is becoming a cheap utility: the cost of a given capability falls about 10x a year, and the value of more intelligence is "super-exponential". Change will feel gradual from the inside (a "gentle singularity"), jobs will change and new ones will appear, and society will need a new social contract, perhaps including a universal "compute budget".

**Dated predictions.** Agents may "join the workforce" in 2025 ([Reflections, January 2025](https://blog.samaltman.com/reflections) [opened]). By 2035 anyone can command intellectual capacity equal to everyone alive in 2025; capital–labor balance "could easily get messed up" ([Three Observations, February 2025](https://blog.samaltman.com/three-observations) [opened]). Systems that produce novel insights in 2026; robots doing real-world tasks in 2027; cost of intelligence converging to the cost of electricity ([The Gentle Singularity, June 2025](https://blog.samaltman.com/the-gentle-singularity) [opened]).

**What happened.** The 2025 agents call mostly failed on its own date: a January 2026 review found no equivalent of coding agents for other work ([Newport](https://calnewport.com/why-didnt-ai-join-the-workforce-in-2025/) [opened]). Within months, coding agents became a multi-billion-dollar product (Claude Code passed $2.5B annualized in February 2026, per [AI Futures](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/q1-2026-timelines-update) [opened]). The 2026 "novel insights" call has support: an unreleased OpenAI model is reported to have disproved a mathematical conjecture ([80,000 Hours](https://80000hours.org/?p=98607) [opened]). His only 2026 blog post followed a firebomb attack on his home and calls for collective, democratic decisions and de-escalation ([blog index](https://blog.samaltman.com/) [opened]).

**Best critique.** The essays are vague where it matters: no numbers for employment, wages or growth, and "new jobs will appear" is asserted by analogy. Zvi Mowshowitz and others read "gentle" as normalizing a transition whose distribution Altman does not specify ([The Zvi](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/the-dream-of-a-gentle-singularity) [secondary]).

### A7. Elon Musk's claims

**Thesis.** AI surpasses any human almost immediately and all humans combined by about 2030; humanoid robots outnumber people; goods and services become nearly free; work becomes optional under "universal high income".

**Dated predictions.** AI smarter than any human by end of 2026, "no later than" 2027, and smarter than all humanity by 2030–2031; Optimus doing complex tasks by end of 2026 and on public sale by end of 2027 ([ForkLog on Davos, January 2026](https://forklog.com/en/news/musk-predicts-optimus-launch-and-agi-emergence-by-2026) [opened]). Earlier: AI smarter than any single human within a year (2024); digital superintelligence by 2026 at the latest (June 2025) ([LessWrong review](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/69qnNx8S7wkSKXJFY/2025-in-ai-predictions) [opened]); 5,000 Optimus units in 2025 ([Electrek](https://electrek.co/2026/08/20/tesla-jpmorgan-fremont-fsd-v15-hw4-optimus-) [opened]).

**What happened.** The 2024 call was graded mostly false. The Optimus 2025 target was missed outright; Musk conceded in January 2026 that none were doing useful work. Tesla's driverless service is a handful of cars in one city more than a year after launch, with about 380,000 unsupervised miles against Waymo's 200M+ (Electrek, as above). Customers were still required to supervise FSD in August 2026 (Electrek, as above).

**Best critique.** The track record. Each of the last three FSD versions was pitched as the one that finishes the job (Electrek). His end-state claims may be right, but his dates carry no information.

## B. Political-economy pessimists

These authors mostly accept that AI will work. Their argument is about who holds power once labor stops being needed. Few give dates, so they are scored on direction, not on numbers.

### B1. "The Intelligence Curse" (Drago and Laine, April 2025)

**Thesis.** States and firms serve ordinary people because they need their labor and taxes. If AI supplies the labor, powerful actors lose that incentive, as rulers of resource-rich states do. Automation arrives through "pyramid replacement": entry-level hiring freezes first, then layoffs moving up the hierarchy, and the end of outlier human talent as a route to mobility. Remedy: avert catastrophes that justify centralization, diffuse AI that augments individuals, and democratize institutions ([intelligence-curse.ai](https://intelligence-curse.ai/) [opened]).

**Predictions.** No dates. The checkable claim is the ordering: hiring freezes at the bottom before layoffs.

**Since then.** The ordering matches the data so far. The Stanford payroll study finds the adjustment is in hiring of 22–25-year-olds, not in separations, and the gap has widened; Fed speakers describe firms slowing hiring instead of firing (yardstick; [Barr, Feb 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/barr20260217a.htm) [opened]). Against: recent-graduate unemployment fell to 5.2% in 2026:Q3.

**Best critique.** The resource-curse analogy may not carry. Oil states are poor at taxing because rents are easy to seize; a democracy with a large AI sector can tax it, and voters keep the franchise even if they lose bargaining power at work. Trammell and Patel make this point: power can rest on the ability to disrupt, not only on being needed ([Capital in the 22nd Century](https://philiptrammell.substack.com/p/capital-in-the-22nd-century) [opened]). The November 2026 sell-off was blamed partly on an election result expected to slow data-center building ([Star-Advertiser/Bloomberg](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/11/05/breaking-news/nasdaq-heads-for-correction-as-ai-stocks-plunge-after-midterms/) [opened]), and 71% of Americans oppose a local AI data center ([State of AI 2026](https://www.stateof.ai/) [opened]); voters still have leverage.

### B2. "Gradual Disempowerment" (Kulveit, Douglas, Ammann, Turan, Krueger, Duvenaud; January 2025)

**Thesis.** No coup or rogue AI is required. The economy, culture and the state all track human interests because they depend on human participation. As AI substitutes for that participation, each system's incentives drift away from people, and the systems reinforce one another: firms with AI-derived wealth shape policy; states funded by AI profits need citizens less. The authors say nobody has a concrete plan to stop it ([gradual-disempowerment.ai](https://gradual-disempowerment.ai/) [opened]).

**Predictions.** None dated or quantified.

**Since then.** Too early to score. Labor's share of income and voter influence have not visibly shifted in two years. An early sign the authors would cite: a lab valued near $1T after growing about 10x a year. Signs against: strong local and electoral pushback (see B1).

**Best critique.** It is hard to falsify, and it treats human institutions as passive. A reader of the 2026 news could tell the opposite story: humans are slowing the build-out through permitting fights and elections.

### B3. Trammell and Patel, "Capital in the 22nd Century" (December 2025)

**Thesis.** Piketty was wrong about the past and probably right about the future. Historically, capital and labor were complements, so accumulation lowered returns and raised wages, which limited inequality. Under full automation capital substitutes for labor, the capital share heads toward one, and wealth concentrates without limit unless redistribution is deliberate. High savers and the patient end up owning most things; catch-up growth for poor countries ends.

**Quantitative claims.** With full automation and equal saving and tax rates, the income Gini would approach about 0.95, against a recorded maximum of 0.63. The US capital share is recently about 44% in nonfarm business. Private firms' share of US corporate capital rose from 8% to 19% since 2000, so the best returns are increasingly closed to small investors. They make no claim about timing ([essay](https://philiptrammell.substack.com/p/capital-in-the-22nd-century) [opened]).

**Policy.** A progressive, internationally coordinated tax on capital or capital income; inheritance taxes; easier pooled access to private markets.

**Since then.** The "privatization of returns" point has grown sharper: the two leading labs are private and valued around $1T each. Economists surveyed by the Forecasting Research Institute expect the top-10% wealth share to rise from 71% to 75% by 2050, and to 80% under rapid AI progress ([FRI, March 2026](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/economic-effects-of-ai) [opened]).

**Best critique.** The result is conditional on full substitutability. If even a few tasks stay human and are complements to the rest, labor's share need not collapse (the Baumol case they set aside). Commenters add that the model assumes humans keep owning capital and keep political power while the economics play out.

### B4. L Rudolf L: "Capital, AGI, and human ambition" (December 2024) and "A History of the Future, 2025–2040" (February 2025)

**Thesis.** Labor-replacing AI makes money far better at buying results, because talent can be cloned and rented. People lose their main lever, selling work, and whoever holds capital at the transition keeps the advantage; social mobility may go "extinct". Material poverty is solvable; loss of leverage is not, and UBI does not restore it. Best case is a richer, static, more unequal Norway ([Capital, AGI, and human ambition](https://www.nosetgauge.com/p/capital-agi-and-human-ambition) [opened]).

**Scenario (summarized in F).** The dated calls for 2025–2026 include: entry-level white-collar hiring gets harder in 2025; code generation "effectively solved" in 2026; SaaS investors nervous; Big Tech headcount still grows; a brief market correction in late 2026 ([Part 1](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/CCnycGceT4HyDKDzK/a-history-of-the-future-2025-2040) [opened]; [Part 2](https://www.nosetgauge.com/p/a-history-of-the-future-2027-2030) [opened]).

**Since then.** These near-term calls look good: entry-level hiring effects, coding agents, software-sector stress (private-credit funds faced redemptions over software exposure, per [Cook, May 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/cook20260527a.htm) [opened]) and a late-2026 correction all occurred. A later reader agreed he was directionally right but questioned where demand for his 2040 industrial output comes from ([Herbie Bradley, via Digg summary](https://digg.com/ai/u2hsdwlq) [secondary]).

**Best critique.** As for B1: it underrates politics. His own scenario has licensed robot-ownership schemes and patronage payouts, which are redistribution by another name.

### B5. Daniel Susskind, "A World Without Work" (2020) and later essays

**Thesis.** "Task encroachment": machines take over tasks one by one, and there is no fixed set of things only humans can do. Technology both substitutes for and complements labor; the complementing force has won so far, but nothing guarantees it will keep winning. The problems that follow are distribution, the power of large technology firms, and meaning. His remedy is a "Big State" that taxes capital and shares income, possibly with conditions.

**Refinement.** Even if AI could do everything more productively, some human work would remain for three reasons: comparative advantage, preferences for human-made or human-delivered things, and moral limits on delegating some decisions. None of the three guarantees enough well-paid work ([What Will Remain for People to Do?, April 2025](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/what-will-remain-for-people-to-do) [opened]). His 2026 Gresham lectures keep the frame and say the challenge is work moving "out of reach", not mass unemployment tomorrow ([Gresham College transcripts](https://www.gresham.ac.uk/sites/default/files/transcript/R_2026_02_17_1232_Susskind_T_FINAL.pdf) [secondary]).

**Predictions.** No dates. The book spoke of the coming decades.

**Since then.** Consistent with a slow version: tasks are being encroached on (coding, translation, support) while employment holds.

**Best critique.** Autor's: the scarce thing in rich countries is workers, not jobs, and the live risk is the devaluation of expertise, not joblessness ([Autor, Noema](https://www.noemamag.com/how-ai-could-help-rebuild-the-middle-class/) [opened]). From the other flank, fast-takeoff writers say "decades" is far too relaxed.

### B6. Yanis Varoufakis, "Technofeudalism" (2023)

**Thesis.** Capitalism has already been replaced. "Cloud capital" (platforms, their algorithms and their data) extracts rent from everyone who must pass through it: "vassal" firms that sell on platforms, workers directed by algorithms, and users who train the systems for nothing. Profit gives way to rent and markets to privately owned fiefs. AI is the same logic intensified. Remedies: a cloud tax, ending "free" services in favor of micropayments, digital rights, and a universal income or dividend not financed by ordinary taxation ([summary of the book's argument in The Friday Times, Nov 2025](https://www.thefridaytimes.com/11-Nov-2025/capitalists-cloudalists-new-techno-feudal-overlords-global-economy) [secondary]).

**Predictions.** None dated.

**Since then.** For: analyst estimates that OpenAI and Anthropic supply 70% or more of the AI revenue of the three big clouds, with investment money circulating between labs and landlords ([Zitron, Aug 2026](https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-ai-demand-bubble/) [opened]). Against: the frontier is a close three-lab race, and the price of a fixed level of capability falls about 13-fold a year ([State of AI 2026](https://www.stateof.ai/) [opened]). That is not how rent behaves.

**Best critique.** Pethokoukis and others argue that generative AI undercuts the thesis: incumbents are being attacked, not entrenched ([The Dispatch](https://thedispatch.com/?p=60019) [secondary]). Marxist critics add that rent has always coexisted with profit, so "feudalism" is a metaphor.

### B7. Yuval Noah Harari

**Thesis.** Industrialization made a working class; AI may make a "useless class", people who are unemployable and so lose political weight along with economic value. Lifelong reinvention is the standard answer, and he doubts most people can do it repeatedly. Later books add that AI can capture culture and politics by mastering language.

**Predictions.** In 2017 he relayed the Frey–Osborne estimate that 47% of US jobs were at high risk by about 2033, and said the 2030–2040 job market was unknowable ([TED Ideas excerpt, 2017](https://ideas.ted.com/the-rise-of-the-useless-class/) [opened]).

**Since then.** Nine years into a twenty-year window, US employment is near record highs. The specific 47% figure looks wrong on timing; the general worry has moved from fringe to mainstream.

**Best critique.** The claim has no mechanism and no date, so it cannot be wrong. Economists object that "useless" confuses market wage with value and ignores demand for human presence.

## C. Skeptics and "normal technology" views

### C1. Narayanan and Kapoor, "AI as Normal Technology" (April 2025)

**Thesis.** AI is a general-purpose technology like electricity, and its effects arrive at the speed of adoption, not invention. Invention, product innovation and diffusion are separate stages; the last takes decades because organizations, laws and workflows must change, and because safety-critical uses need reliability that benchmarks do not measure. "Superintelligence" is a confused target; what matters is power, and people can keep control with ordinary engineering and regulation. Policy should build resilience and avoid drastic measures.

**Quantitative claims.** Diffusion happens "over decades"; in August 2024, 40% of US adults used generative AI but it accounted for only 0.5–3.5% of work hours ([essay](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology) [opened]).

**Since then.** For: firm adoption was 17% in December 2025; unemployment and productivity are ordinary; most firms are still piloting (yardstick). Against: lab revenue tripled in eight months, and the authors' own stated exception, a discontinuity from recursive self-improvement, is now a live empirical question. Narayanan restated in 2026 that the frame holds unless such a discontinuity occurs ([report of ICML 2026 keynote](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/arvind-narayanan-at-icml-2026-ai-is-normal-technology) [secondary]); METR now estimates roughly 2x acceleration at one lab.

**Best critique.** Past technologies could not help deploy themselves. If agents can do the integration work that makes diffusion slow, the historical speed limit may not bind. Critics also say the essay works better as a goal than as a forecast ([LessWrong](https://lesswrong.com/posts/3eD7Bab5kArqkdrQs/ai-as-a-normal-technology-should-be-a-vision) [secondary]).

### C2. Daron Acemoglu

**Thesis.** Aggregate gains equal the share of tasks affected times the cost saving on each, and both are small for now. Only about 5% of tasks are profitably automatable in the near term. AI is being pointed at replacing workers when it should augment them, and the hype misallocates capital.

**Quantitative claims.** At most 0.66% more total factor productivity over ten years, under 0.53% after adjusting for hard tasks; AI will widen the capital–labor income gap ([The Simple Macroeconomics of AI, NBER 2024](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487) [opened]). In June 2026 he put it at about 0.55% of TFP and 1–1.5% of GDP over a decade, said big gains would need something close to AGI, and warned that if 30–40% of new graduates could not find work the political consequences would be severe ([Fortune, 21 June 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/06/21/nobel-laureate-daron-acemoglu-ai-productivity-capitalism-democracy/) [opened]).

**Since then.** Measured productivity is consistent with a small effect so far, and Fed officials attribute recent strength mainly to other causes (Barr, Feb 2026). But $105B of lab run-rate revenue, mostly for coding and enterprise use, sits awkwardly with "5% of tasks", and capability growth has not slowed.

**Best critique.** The method holds capabilities fixed at roughly 2023 levels and excludes new tasks and faster science by construction. Epoch's authors say they are "increasingly puzzled" by confident low estimates ([Potlogea and Ho](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/ai-and-explosive-growth-redux) [opened]). Penn Wharton, using his framework with adoption growing over time, gets two to three times his number (see D).

### C3. David Autor

**Thesis.** The danger is not too few jobs; rich countries are short of workers. The question is what happens to the value of expertise. Computers concentrated decision-making in an elite; AI could reverse that by letting trained non-elite workers do higher-stakes tasks, as nurse practitioners did. He calls this a scenario, not a forecast, and says it depends on choices ([Noema, February 2024](https://www.noemamag.com/how-ai-could-help-rebuild-the-middle-class/) [opened]). A 2026 paper with Acemoglu and Johnson defines "pro-worker AI" as technology that makes human skills more valuable ([NBER w34854](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34854/w34854.pdf) [secondary]).

**Predictions.** None dated.

**Since then.** Mixed. Experiments keep showing the largest gains for the least experienced ([Barr, July 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/barr20260714a.htm) [opened]). But in the labor market it is the least experienced who are not being hired.

**Best critique.** The nurse-practitioner analogy needs the AI to stay a tool that requires a trained human. If the model can do the whole task, the middle-skill worker is not needed. Half of surveyed US economists now expect AI to hurt the earnings of many high-skilled workers within ten years ([Clark Center, May 2026](https://kentclarkcenter.org/surveys/ai-work-and-education/) [opened]).

### C4. Tyler Cowen on bottlenecks

**Thesis.** He is bullish on capability and bearish on speed. Slow sectors (government, health, education) grow as a share of the economy as fast sectors get cheap. The more productive AI becomes, the more the remaining human steps set the pace, as in an O-ring production chain. Diffusion has always been slower than clever observers expect. Market prices are not forecasting a rapid transformation.

**Quantitative claim.** AI adds about half a percentage point to annual growth, which he calls a guess ([Marginal Revolution, February 2025](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2025/02/why-i-think-ai-take-off-is-relatively-slow.html) [opened]).

**Since then.** Nothing in the macro data contradicts +0.5 points. Real interest rates have risen a great deal since he wrote (see E), though for mixed reasons.

**Best critique.** The cost-disease argument assumes the slow sectors stay closed to AI. If AI can do the regulators' and administrators' work too, or if new firms route around incumbents, the bottleneck moves. Barnett's reply is that even with poor substitutability, automating remote work alone doubles output ([Barnett](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/consequences-of-automating-remote-work) [opened]).

### C5. Noah Smith on comparative advantage

**Thesis.** Even if AI is better at everything, humans can keep well-paid work, because AI capacity is finite and will be put to its most valuable uses, leaving other tasks to people. This holds if the constraint on AI (compute) is specific to AI. It fails if AI and humans compete for the same scarce inputs, energy and land, in which case humans could go the way of horses.

**Update.** In a March 2026 repost he stresses that he argued this is possible, not guaranteed; says it implies some limits on data-center resource use; and cites Korinek and Suh's result that wages can be squeezed into fewer tasks and then partly collapse ([Noahpinion, 28 March 2026](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/plentiful-high-paying-jobs-in-the-ff9) [opened]).

**Since then.** Jobs remain plentiful, which fits but does not test the claim: AI is nowhere near better at everything. The resource-competition branch has become concrete. Electricity and water prices each rose about 5% in a year, and a Fed governor links the pressure to AI-driven demand ([Cook, Sept 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/cook20260928a.htm) [opened]).

**Best critique.** Comparative advantage guarantees trade, not a living wage. If compute keeps getting cheaper, the wage at which a human is worth using instead of more compute can fall below subsistence ([Philosophy Bear](https://philosophybear.substack.com/p/portubots-and-englifleshes-why-comparative) [secondary]).

### C6. Gary Marcus

**Thesis.** Large language models are unreliable pattern-matchers; scaling them does not produce general intelligence; neurosymbolic methods and world models are needed. The industry's economics do not work and valuations will fall.

**Dated predictions.** For 2025: no AGI; no system passes more than 4 of the Marcus–Brundage tasks; agents hyped but unreliable; humanoids hyped; driverless cars limited to a modest number of cities; under 10% of the workforce replaced; profits modest or absent; (medium confidence) valuations may start to fall; (low confidence) no GPT-5-level leap ([25 predictions for 2025](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/25-ai-predictions-for-2025-from-marcus) [opened]). For 2026: no AGI in 2026 or 2027; home robots mostly demos; 2025 remembered as "peak bubble"; AI becomes a midterm election issue ([predictions for 2026](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/six-or-seven-predictions-for-ai-2026) [opened]).

**Since then.** He scores himself 16 of 17 on the high-confidence 2025 list, and outside reviewers agree on the main items ([LessWrong review](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/69qnNx8S7wkSKXJFY/2025-in-ai-predictions) [opened]). For 2026: robots-as-demos and the midterm call look right (the 5 November sell-off was attributed to the election result). "Peak bubble in 2025" looks wrong on timing: OpenAI went from a $500B valuation in October 2025 ([AI Futures](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/grading-ai-2027s-2025-predictions) [opened]) to near $1T by mid-2026, Anthropic reached $965B in May 2026, and chip stocks peaked in June 2026. The capability-plateau thesis has not held: task horizons are doubling faster than before.

**Best critique.** His predictions are mostly of the form "X will not happen this year", which is safe when the calendar is short and says little about the trend. He has been right about reliability and wrong about usefulness and demand.

### C7. Yann LeCun

**Thesis.** Language models manipulate text well but have no model of the world, so they cannot plan or reason about consequences; scaling them to human level is an illusion. Human-level AI will come from architectures that learn abstract world models from video and sensor data, and that needs conceptual breakthroughs.

**Predictions.** Human-level AI not in the next year or two; it will "take a while" ([MIT Technology Review, 22 January 2026](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/01/22/1131661/yann-lecuns-new-venture-ami-labs/) [opened]). He left Meta and raised about $1B for AMI Labs on this bet ([search summary](https://www.eweek.com/news/meta-ai-yann-lecun-ami-world-models-startup/) [secondary]).

**Since then.** His near-term negative (no human-level AI by 2027) is on track. His implied claim that language-model systems would stall on agentic work is not: they run for most of a working day and speed up AI research. His critique of humanoid robots built on scripted motion matches the robotics record.

**Best critique.** Whether the architecture is "really" reasoning matters less to the economy than whether it does the task. A system that completes 41% of sampled freelance projects is economically relevant with or without a world model.

### C8. Andrej Karpathy, "decade of agents" (October 2025)

**Thesis.** Agents lack memory, continual learning, multimodality and reliable computer use; fixing these is tractable but takes about ten years. AGI will not appear as a break in the GDP series, any more than computers or phones did; the "intelligence explosion" is the long automation trend we are already in ([Dwarkesh podcast, 17 October 2025](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/andrej-karpathy) [opened]). FRI records his date for automated AI research as about 2035 ([FRI, Nov 2026](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/recursive-self-improvement-v1) [opened]).

**Since then.** The mood he described reversed within two months when coding agents began completing long independent projects ([80,000 Hours](https://80000hours.org/?p=98607) [opened]). His missing pieces are still missing: METR finds no large gain in research "taste", and continual learning is unsolved. His macro claim (no visible break) holds so far.

**Best critique.** Smooth aggregate curves are compatible with violent sectoral change, and "no break yet" is what both camps predict for 2026.

### C9. Dwarkesh Patel's timeline essays

**Thesis.** The binding constraint is continual learning: models do not get better at a job by doing it, so they cannot replace employees however capable each session is. Timelines are lognormal: compute scale-up cannot continue past about 2030, so it is "this decade or bust".

**Dated predictions (June 2025).** 50% that AI can do his small business's taxes end to end by 2028; 50% on human-like on-the-job learning by 2032; reliable computer-use agents by end-2026 "likely too optimistic"; if progress stopped, under 25% of white-collar jobs would go ([essay](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/timelines-june-2025) [opened]).

**Since then.** Open. The Remote Labor Index figure suggests general computer work is moving faster than he implied, while lab staff and METR still name judgment and learning as the gap. I could not find a 2026 timelines essay by him; his 2026 output is interviews on recursive self-improvement and compute ([archive](https://www.dwarkesh.com/archive) [opened]).

**Best critique.** Greenblatt's reply in the comments: the gap is quantitative, and reinforcement learning with self-checking can stand in for human-style learning. The 2026 gains came from scaling, not from solving his blocker ([80,000 Hours](https://80000hours.org/?p=98607) [opened]).

### C10. Ed Zitron and other bubble arguments

**Thesis.** AI demand is two money-losing labs funded by the same hyperscalers that book their spending as cloud revenue. Outside them, demand is small. Data centers are being overbuilt, and the unwinding will hit the whole market.

**Numbers (August 2026).** Analysts put OpenAI and Anthropic at 70% or more of the big three clouds' AI revenue; non-lab AI compute demand under $30B; Microsoft's non-lab AI revenue about $19B against $186B of capex; hyperscalers likely to restate revenue and admit overcapacity "in the next year or two" ([The AI Demand Bubble](https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-ai-demand-bubble/) [opened]). He cites audited OpenAI losses of $20.9B in 2025 ([search summary](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/zitron-70-of-ai-revenue-traces-to-openai-and-anthropic) [secondary]).

**Since then.** In this piece he does not dispute the labs' revenue growth, though he distrusts "annualized" figures; the argument rests on profitability and circularity. No collapse has occurred, but the infrastructure layer has repriced hard: CoreWeave is 65% below its high, Oracle 51%, Intel 59%, the semiconductor index 38% ([Trefis](https://www.trefis.com/articles/622421/what-s-happening-with-ai-stocks/2026-11-05) [opened]). Anthropic reported positive adjusted operating income in 2026:Q2 and inference gross margins above 70% (Channel Insider; 80,000 Hours), which cuts against "unprofitable at any scale".

**Best critique.** Concentration of demand in the firms selling the product to end users is what an early platform market looks like; end-user demand is real if the labs' revenue is. The finance can be a bubble while the technology is not, as with railways and fiber.

### C11. Goldman Sachs, "Gen AI: too much spend, too little benefit?" (June 2024)

**Thesis.** Jim Covello: about $1T of capex needs AI to solve complex, high-value problems to earn a return, and it is not built for that; past technologies started cheap, this one starts expensive. The same report carried Acemoglu's small-gain estimate and Goldman's own optimists ([Goldman Sachs](https://goldmansachs.com/insights/top-of-mind/gen-ai-too-much-spend-too-little-benefit) [secondary]; the site blocked my fetch).

**Prediction.** In 2024 he gave the spending 18 months to two years to show a payoff (as recalled in the Fortune piece below).

**Since then.** Two years on, Covello says firms are losing more money implementing AI than two years ago and that returns are further away; he concedes consumer adoption far exceeded his expectations and that the technology advanced quickly; he now prefers hyperscalers to chip makers because chip firms capture nearly all the value. A colleague estimates $7–8T will ultimately be spent ([Fortune, 5 June 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/06/05/is-ai-a-bubble-worth-it-short-term-early-goldman-skeptic-covello-profits/) [opened]).

**Best critique.** The 2024 report underestimated demand by a wide margin. The remaining claim, that buyers are not yet earning a return, is about distribution of the surplus, and coding tools have been cheap and useful in the way he said the technology was not.

### C12. The MIT "95% of pilots fail" report and its critics

**Claim.** MIT's NANDA project reported in August 2025 that about 5% of enterprise generative-AI pilots produced rapid revenue gains and the rest showed little measurable profit impact. It also found that bought tools succeed about twice as often as internal builds, that the best returns were in back-office work, and that firms were not backfilling roles more than laying off. It blamed a "learning gap": tools that do not adapt to workflows ([Fortune, 18 Aug 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo/) [opened]).

**Critiques.** The definition counts any pilot not in full production with measured profit impact as a failure, including pilots that correctly found an idea unworkable; the sample is small (150 interviews, 350 survey responses, 300 public deployments; one critic says 52 organizations); the method behind the 95% is not disclosed; the window was short ([Paid's critique](https://arnon.dk/mits-95-ai-failure-rate-is-wrong/) [opened], written by an AI vendor with its own interest; [Werbach and others](https://www.govinfosecurity.com/most-ai-pilots-never-take-flight-a-29361) [secondary]).

**Since then.** The number is still quoted (Fortune's June 2026 profile of Covello repeats it). Census data showing 17% firm adoption and most firms piloting fits the direction if not the figure. The report's own "learning gap" diagnosis is the same continual-learning point Patel and Karpathy make.

**Best reading.** Weak evidence for a true statement (enterprise integration is slow) and no evidence for the stronger one (the technology does not work).

### Federal Reserve officials, 2025–2026

- **Barr, May 2025.** Two scenarios: incremental (most jobs change, employment stays high, real wages rise) and transformational (AI takes a broad range of cognitive and manual jobs, employment and participation fall, the labor share drops sharply). Either raises the neutral interest rate ([speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/barr20250509a.htm) [opened]).
- **Barr, February 2026.** Adds a third: gradual adoption; a "jobless boom" with fast displacement and falling participation; and stalled progress, where the main risk moves to the financial sector. He says the evidence so far is more consistent with the first, that monetary policy cannot fix structural unemployment, that he has raised his estimate of the neutral rate modestly, and that the AI boom is unlikely to be a reason to cut rates ([speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/barr20260217a.htm) [opened]).
- **Barr, July 2026.** On inequality: he does not know which way AI cuts. Graduate-degree holders use AI four times as much as those with high school or less (43% against 10%); returns could concentrate in a few firms; competition and tax policy are named as tools outside the Fed's remit ([speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/barr20260714a.htm) [opened]).
- **Cook, May 2026.** AI-related job loss could come before job gains; the debt-financed build-out could become a financial-stability concern, though leverage is far below 2007; software-exposed private credit has already seen redemption pressure ([speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/cook20260527a.htm) [opened]).
- **Cook, September 2026.** In the short run AI is inflationary (chips, power, construction labor, wealth-driven spending); in a few years it should be modestly disinflationary; the medium-term labor risk is a "painful transition"; unemployment and layoffs have been flat for two years while adoption rose. She voted to raise rates ([speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/cook20260928a.htm) [opened]).
- **Waller, November 2026.** The market is roughly balanced but lacks churn; Beige Book contacts report delaying hires after AI investments; AI is one of several reasons for cautious hiring ([speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/waller20261110a.htm) [opened]).
- **Jefferson, April 2026.** Treats AI only as investment demand and a possible productivity support ([speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/jefferson20260407a.htm) [opened]).

**Scoring the institutions.** On the macro variables they forecast, they are right so far: nothing in unemployment, growth or productivity is outside their bands. On the inputs, they have been overtaken: none anticipated lab revenue tripling in eight months, $733B of capex in a year, or measurable AI acceleration of AI research.

**Best critique.** Their method cannot produce a large number. It multiplies today's exposure by today's cost savings and an adoption curve borrowed from the PC, so it is a forecast of the first decade of a normal technology. The Forecasting Research Institute finds that economists' own conditional forecasts barely move even when they are told to assume rapid AI progress (see E), which suggests the conservatism is in the model of the economy, not in beliefs about the technology.

## E. What markets and forecasters imply

### Real interest rates

**The argument.** If investors expected explosive growth (or extinction) within a few decades, they would want to consume more now and save less, and long-term real interest rates would be far above normal. Chow, Halperin and Mazlish made this case in January 2023, when 30-year real rates were under 2%: either transformative AI is not coming within 30 years or markets are wrong ([agiandtheemh.com](https://www.agiandtheemh.com/) [opened]). In their baseline, 30% growth implies real rates near 30% ([search summary of the paper](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/8c7LycgtkypkgYjZx/agi-and-the-emh-markets-are-not-expecting-aligned-or) [secondary]).

**The level now.** The 10-year TIPS yield is 2.66% and the 30-year 3.08% (9 November 2026) ([FRED DFII10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DFII10) [opened]; [DFII30](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DFII30) [opened]). A July 2026 10-year TIPS auction cleared at 2.438%, the highest for that maturity since October 2008, with the nominal 10-year at about 4.7% and breakeven inflation at 2.26% ([TIPS Watch](https://tipswatch.com/2026/07/23/10-year-tips-auction-gets-real-yield-of-2-438-a-great-result-for-investors/) [opened]). The 10-year real yield touched about 2.9% in early October ([search summary](https://www.thetrading.tools/real-yields) [secondary]). The 30-year real rate was about 1.6% at the end of 2022 (same secondary source as the paper).

**What it says.** Long real rates are about 1.5 points higher than when the argument was made and are at post-2008 highs. That is the direction the theory predicts. But:
- The level, about 3%, is what you would expect from somewhat faster trend growth and heavy borrowing. It is nowhere near what a market expecting 10–30% growth would demand.
- Other causes are sufficient. The TIPS Watch author attributes the rise to Middle East tensions and to "massive" debt issuance by the US government and by AI-building corporations. Inflation is 3.7%, and Fed governors are talking about rate increases, not cuts.
- Fed governors say AI investment is pushing up the neutral rate modestly (Barr, February 2026).

So the bond market is pricing an investment boom and perhaps a point of extra growth. It is not pricing a transformation. Critics of the method note that the link from growth to rates weakens if the gains go to people who save most of their income, which is exactly what Trammell and Patel expect ([Decker](https://nicholasdecker.substack.com/p/will-transformative-ai-really-raise) [secondary]; [Trammell and Patel](https://philiptrammell.substack.com/p/capital-in-the-22nd-century) [opened]).

### AI equity valuations: was there a correction in 2026?

Yes, more than one, and the latest is in progress.

- The Nasdaq 100 entered its third correction of 2026 in early November. On 5 November it fell more than 6%, its worst day since March 2020, leaving it nearly 15% below its 6 October record with $2.3T of value erased. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12% on the day and is more than 38% below its 22 June peak. The VIX closed at 28 ([Star-Advertiser/Bloomberg](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/11/05/breaking-news/nasdaq-heads-for-correction-as-ai-stocks-plunge-after-midterms/) [opened]).
- The damage is concentrated in infrastructure: from 52-week highs, CoreWeave −65%, Intel −59%, Oracle −51%, Marvell −49%, Alphabet −28%, AMD −25%, Nvidia −16%. Apple and Microsoft were at highs. The S&P 500 was still up about 12% for the year and Nvidia was down about 7% ([Trefis](https://www.trefis.com/articles/622421/what-s-happening-with-ai-stocks/2026-11-05) [opened]).
- Stated causes: a Democratic midterm win read as a threat to data-center build-out, hedge-fund deleveraging, a 10-year yield near 4.9%, and worry about payback on capex, not weak demand (Amazon and Alphabet said demand exceeds capacity; AMD guided to 80% revenue growth in 2027).
- Trefis's rough odds: 55% a pullback that holds, 35% a 2022-style bear market, 10% a 2000-style bust.
- Private valuations went the other way during 2026: Anthropic at $965B in May; OpenAI near $1T (yardstick; Fortune, June 2026).

**Reading.** Equity markets price AI as a very large industry whose profits are uncertain and whose suppliers were overbought. That is a different bet from the bond market's and a much smaller one than the takeoff camp's: even a $1T lab is priced for tens of billions in profit, not for owning most of the economy.

### Expert surveys

- **AI researchers (AI Impacts, 2,778 respondents, late 2023).** 50% chance of machines outperforming humans at every task by 2047, thirteen years earlier than the same survey a year before; 50% chance of all occupations being fully automatable by 2116 ([arXiv 2401.02843](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843) [opened]). The 70-year gap between those two answers is the diffusion crux in survey form.
- **FRI's Longitudinal Expert AI Panel (2026).** Median year in which experts would agree AGI has arrived: 2050, with a range of roughly 2032 to 2079. US labor-force participation in 2029: 61.7% ([LEAP](https://leap.forecastingresearch.org/) [opened]).
- **FRI, "Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI" (fielded October 2025 to February 2026; 69 economists, 52 AI professionals, 38 superforecasters).** Economists give 14% to "rapid" AI progress by 2030 (superforecasters 12.6%). Unconditional medians for 2030: GDP growth 2.5%, unemployment 5.0%, participation 61.0%. Under the rapid scenario: growth about 3.3–3.5%, unemployment 6.0%, participation 59.3% in 2030 and 55.0% in 2050, labor share 45% and top-10% wealth share 80% by 2050. AI professionals expect 8% unemployment and a 40% labor share in 2050 under rapid progress. Policy: 72% of economists back retraining support, 14% a job guarantee (57% of the public), and a plurality oppose UBI (the public favors it 48–30). The paper's main finding is that experts disagree mostly about what capable AI does to the economy, not about how fast AI improves ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/economic-effects-of-ai) [opened]).
- **Clark Center US economists panel (13 May 2026).** "AI will substantially raise unemployment in advanced countries within ten years": 12% agree, 27% disagree, 61% uncertain. "AI will hurt the earnings of substantial numbers of high-skilled workers": 51% agree, 15% disagree. "AI makes returns to education substantially more uncertain": 66% agree ([Clark Center](https://kentclarkcenter.org/surveys/ai-work-and-education/) [opened]; percentages computed from individual votes). In 2017 the same panel was more worried: 43% agreed robots and AI would substantially raise long-term unemployment if institutions stayed fixed, and 89% agreed the gains could compensate losers ([2017 poll](https://kentclarkcenter.org/surveys/robots-and-artificial-intelligence-2/) [opened]).
- **Threshold 2030 (Convergence Analysis, Metaculus, FLI; conference late 2024, report February 2025).** Thirty economists and forecasters worked through three capability scenarios for 2030; the findings are in a PDF I could not open ([Convergence](https://www.convergenceanalysis.org/threshold-2030) [opened]).

**How good are these forecasters?** They have run behind events on AI itself. The AI 2027 authors report that a forecaster survey under-predicted lab revenue by about 2x ([AI Futures](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/grading-ai-2027s-2025-predictions) [opened]); the AI Impacts median moved 13 years in one year; FRI published an editorial in September 2026 on improving the accuracy of its AI forecasts ([FRI research index](https://forecastingresearch.org/research) [opened]). They have not been tested on the economic variables, because nothing unusual has happened to those yet.

## F. Explicit scenario narratives

### F1. AI 2027 (April 2025), two endings
**Assumptions.** One lab automates AI research in 2027; research speed multiplies by tens; alignment is unsolved; the US and China race; the economy follows capability with a lag of about a year.
**Path.** 2025: unreliable agents. 2026: $1T of AI capex, market +30%, junior coders squeezed. 2027: superhuman coder (March), superhuman researcher, a public backlash, government oversight. Then:
- *Race:* 2028, AI-run special economic zones, a million robots a month, "stratospheric" GDP and tax revenue, job loss managed so smoothly people accept it. 2029, humans economically obsolete, generous UBI, the Dow above one million. 2030, AI kills nearly everyone.
- *Slowdown:* compute is consolidated under the Defense Production Act; 2028, both parties promise basic income, robots scale; 2029, poverty largely ended, trillionaires, most revenue from taxing or nationalizing AI firms, control held by a small oversight committee.
([ai-2027.com/race](https://ai-2027.com/race) [opened]; [ai-2027.com/slowdown](https://ai-2027.com/slowdown) [opened])
**Status.** Running at about 75% of pace on capabilities, ahead on revenue, behind on stock prices; authors' medians now late 2027 to 2030 for the coding milestone.

### F2. L Rudolf L, "A History of the Future, 2025–2040" (February 2025)
**Assumptions.** No fast intelligence explosion; compute-heavy experiments limit self-improvement; diffusion and politics matter; alignment mostly holds; robotics is limited by real-world data until the 2030s.
**Path.**
- 2025–2026: agents real but supervised; entry-level hiring tightens; code generation "effectively solved"; a brief market correction in late 2026.
- 2027–2030: a $500-a-month model does much office work; software prices collapse; office jobs become a few hours of AI oversight; firms stop hiring and do not replace retirees but avoid mass firings; EU "human in the loop" laws create sinecures; humanoids above $50,000 until a cheap-car-priced household robot ships about 15,000 units in 2029; no UBI because the country is not yet rich enough.
- 2030–2040: US growth 5% a year in 2031–2032, a Social Security funding crisis, a licensed robot-ownership scheme in 2033; robot labor grows about 4x a year, from 40M humanoids in 2033 to billions by 2036; Europe pays nearly everyone through patronage by 2036; poor countries lose services exports and stagnate; advancement by merit is "almost extinct" by the mid-2030s; by 2040 manufacturing output is over 1,000 times 2025 and humans own assets they no longer steer.
([Part 1](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/CCnycGceT4HyDKDzK/a-history-of-the-future-2025-2040) [opened]; [Part 2](https://www.nosetgauge.com/p/a-history-of-the-future-2027-2030) [opened]; [Part 3](https://www.nosetgauge.com/p/a-history-of-the-future-2030-2040) [opened])
**Status.** The 2025–2026 calls are among the best in this file.

### F3. Citrini Research, "The 2028 Global Intelligence Crisis" (22 February 2026)
**Assumptions.** Firms use AI to cut white-collar payroll, not to expand; white-collar workers are half of employment and three quarters of discretionary spending; displaced workers trade down and compress wages elsewhere; mortgages and private credit assume stable professional incomes; tax revenue depends on wages; nothing automatically stops the loop. The authors call it a thought exercise, not a prediction.
**Path.** 2026: coding agents let firms rebuild SaaS in-house; software firms cut staff; the S&P nears 8,000 in October; job openings fall below 5.5M; the 10-year yield falls from 4.3% to 3.2%. 2027: consumer agents strip out intermediaries (travel, insurance, real-estate commissions, card fees); recession from Q2; jobless claims at 487,000; a record private-credit software default; a November crash. 2028: unemployment 10.2%, S&P down 38% from its peak, tech-city house prices down about 10%, federal receipts 12% below baseline, proposals for a compute tax and a public stake in AI returns.
([Citrini](https://www.citriniresearch.com/p/2028gic) [opened])
**Status.** Its 2026 leg was right on the sector (software stress, private-credit redemptions) and wrong on the macro sign: yields rose to about 4.9%, inflation rose, Fed governors turned toward tightening, and openings ticked up (Cook, September). So far AI has been a demand boom, not a demand shortfall.

### F4. Korinek, and Korinek and Suh (2023–2024)
**Assumptions.** Work is a set of tasks ordered by complexity; automation climbs that ladder; wages depend on a race between automation and capital accumulation.
**Path.** Three scenarios: business as usual (displaced workers move to new tasks); AGI in 20 years; AGI in 5 years. In both AGI cases output growth accelerates sharply because labor stops being the scarce factor. Wages rise at first while labor is still scarce, then collapse as the last tasks are automated; they can fall earlier if automation outruns capital. If there is always an unautomated tail of tasks, wages can rise indefinitely. Recommended policy is a portfolio: stress-test tax and benefit systems against each scenario and start a small basic income that can be scaled.
([NBER w32255](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32255) [opened]; [IMF Finance & Development, December 2023](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/fandd/issues/2023/12/Scenario-Planning-for-an-AGI-future-Anton-korinek) [opened])
**Status.** Early phase only: wages are still rising.

### F5. Federal Reserve scenarios
Barr's three (gradual; jobless boom; stall) and the Dallas Fed's four (trend; +0.3 points; benign singularity; extinction) are in section D. Assumption: the fork is the speed of capability and adoption. Barr's judgment in February 2026 was that the gradual path fits the evidence.

### F6. Kokotajlo, "What 2026 looks like" (written August 2021)
**Assumptions.** Scaling language models plus better scaffolding; no dramatic breakthroughs; his median for transformative AI was then about 2029.
**Path.** 2022–2023: chatbots are fun, revenue recoups training costs, self-driving does not arrive. 2024: hype fades, any GDP effect is too small to detect, persuasion tools spread. 2025: agentic systems built on pretrained models reach expert level in games like Diplomacy. 2026: assistants finally work for serious economic tasks, hype and stock-market enthusiasm return, a new chip shortage, some jobs lost in the pattern of past technologies, and still no acceleration in world GDP.
([Alignment Forum](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/6Xgy6CAf2jqHhynHL/what-2026-looks-like) [opened])
**Status.** The economic line for 2026 is close to exact. He overestimated AI propaganda and censorship, by his own later account.

### F7. Forethought's industrial explosion (2025)
Staged, not dated: about 10x physical output from AI-directed human labor; then self-replicating robot factories with a doubling time near a year, shortening over 1–5 doublings; then nanotechnology. Assumes abundant AI cognition and no binding political limit ([Forethought](https://www.forethought.org/research/the-industrial-explosion) [opened]).

### F8. Epoch's GATE (2025)
A model, not a story: growth of 2–20 times the historical rate through an automation decade, AI investment above 10% of world output, most tasks automatable within two decades under conservative settings. Assumes disembodied AI substitutes perfectly for labor and investors are certain of full automation ([Epoch](https://epoch.ai/blog/announcing-gate) [opened]).

### F9. Amodei's two essays (2024, 2026)
Powerful AI within a few years; a "compressed century" of biology in 5–10 years; 10–20% growth possible; half of entry-level white-collar jobs displaced in 1–5 years; trillion-dollar fortunes; progressive taxation and philanthropy as the response (A5).

### F10. David Shapiro's "post-labor economics"
**Framework.** Most labor is "derived demand": people want the outcome, not the worker, so anything a machine does better, faster, cheaper and safer will be automated, and every organization is pushed to do so. Lasting demand for humans is confined to four narrow categories: presence (a human performing in person), provenance (made by a human), liability (a human who can be held accountable, the "statutory economy") and affinity (a particular named person). These are a small, inelastic slice of spending, so wages cannot remain the main way income is distributed ([Shapiro, 20 Sept 2026](https://daveshap.substack.com/p/most-jobs-are-going-away) [opened]; [archive](https://daveshap.substack.com/archive) [opened]).
**Path and dates.** The 2026 posts I could open give none; the fuller treatment is a video and a forthcoming book, *Labor Zero*, which I could not read.
**Best critique.** The four categories are asserted to be small without measurement. Cowen's cost-disease argument points the other way: the sectors where presence and liability matter most (health, education, government) take a growing share of the economy as everything else gets cheap ([Cowen](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2025/02/why-i-think-ai-take-off-is-relatively-slow.html) [opened]).

### F11. Policy-planning bodies
- **Windfall Trust.** Runs scenario exercises (including a UK preparedness analysis and a UN tabletop in September 2026), a "Policy Atlas" of 48 options (wage insurance, bargaining rights, tax credits), and proposals for a global dividend and a progressive global corporate tax. I did not open the scenario documents ([windfalltrust.org](https://www.windfalltrust.org/) [opened]).
- **Convergence Analysis.** Threshold 2030 (above) and the "AGI Social Contract" essay series ([convergenceanalysis.org](https://www.convergenceanalysis.org/) [opened]).
- **Brookings.** I found no year-by-year scenario. Its contribution is measurement: with the Yale Budget Lab it found in October 2025 that the occupational mix had not shifted unusually in 33 months since ChatGPT and that unemployment was not concentrated among the AI-exposed, with the caveat that early-career effects could be an early signal ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/new-data-show-no-ai-jobs-apocalypse-for-now/) [opened]).

### F12. FRI's conditional forecasts
Economists' slow, moderate and rapid scenarios for 2030 and 2050 (section E) are the nearest thing to a consensus scenario table: even the rapid case has 3.5% growth and 6% unemployment.

### Who has been best calibrated so far?

No camp has been right on both halves. The record splits cleanly:

1. **On capability, revenue and investment, the short-timeline camp has been closest, and nearly everyone else was too low.** Aschenbrenner's 2024 investment and compute numbers were met or beaten. AI 2027 was too fast on benchmarks by a quarter to a third and too slow on revenue. Skeptics who predicted a plateau, weak demand or a 2025 bust were wrong on each. Professional forecasters under-predicted lab revenue by half.
2. **On unemployment, growth and productivity, the gradualists have been right.** Narayanan and Kapoor, Cowen, the Fed's "gradual" scenario and the institutional models all said the macro data would look ordinary through 2026, and they do.
3. **The best single forecasts are the ones that held both views at once.** Kokotajlo's 2021 vignette had AI working for serious economic tasks by 2026 with no GDP acceleration. L Rudolf L's 2025 scenario had coding solved, entry-level hiring squeezed, firms not replacing leavers, and a late-2026 market wobble. Erdil and Barnett's "broad, gradual, salient" picture of automation fits the labor data, though they were too cautious on revenue. All three assume capability outruns diffusion.
4. **Worst calibrated:** Musk on every dated claim; the "AGI in 2025–2026" cluster; "bubble pops in 2025"; and any forecast of mass white-collar job loss within months.
5. **A caution about the gradualists' win.** They have been right about lagging indicators during the period when both camps predicted little macro change. The fast camp's distinctive claims (automated AI research, then the economy) fall due in 2027–2030. The gradualists' own stated failure condition, AI materially speeding AI research, has moved from hypothetical to "plausibly 2x" in a year.

## H. The real cruxes

The Forecasting Research Institute's finding is the best one-line summary: experts disagree less about how fast AI improves than about what capable AI does to the economy ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/economic-effects-of-ai) [opened]). Six underlying disagreements generate nearly all the conclusions above.

### 1. How long until AI can do all remote cognitive work, including AI research?
- **Short (2027–2030):** AI Futures, Aschenbrenner, Amodei, lab roadmaps (OpenAI targets an automated researcher by March 2028; Anthropic says early 2027 is plausible). Evidence: task horizons doubling every four months, 41% on the Remote Labor Index, roughly 2x research acceleration at one lab.
- **Long (2035–2050 or later):** Karpathy (about 2035), Erdil (median about 20 years), the LEAP panel (2050), LeCun (new architectures needed). Evidence: no on-the-job learning, weak research judgment, poor results when models run a messy business, saturated benchmarks that measure clean tasks.
- **What would settle it:** whether a lab's research staff shrinks or its progress accelerates past 2x in 2027; whether agents handle week-long, ill-specified work.
- **What follows:** everything else. If the answer is "long", the institutional forecasts in D are roughly right for the 2026–2036 decade.

### 2. Does automated research feed on itself, or hit compute and experiment limits?
- **Feeds on itself:** Forethought (returns to software R&D may exceed one), AI 2027.
- **Hits limits:** Erdil and Barnett (ideas need compute-heavy experiments; a software-only loop fizzles within an order of magnitude), Narayanan and Kapoor.
- **Evidence so far:** 1.3x–2.8x at Anthropic, which METR cannot yet tell apart from a one-off jump; Google DeepMind attributes its own speed-up mainly to more inputs ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/recursive-self-improvement-v1) [opened]).
- **What follows:** whether superhuman capability arrives within a year or two of crux 1, or over a decade or more.

### 3. How fast does capability become deployed production?
- **Decades:** Narayanan and Kapoor, Cowen, Acemoglu, the institutional models. Organizations, liability, regulation and trust set the pace; 17% of firms use AI; most are piloting.
- **Years or less:** the labs and Epoch's remote-work argument. Agents can do their own integration; new firms can be built around AI; the fastest revenue ramp on record is diffusion.
- **The gap in one number:** AI researchers put a 50% chance on machines beating humans at every task by 2047 and on full automation of occupations by 2116.
- **What follows:** whether there is a long period in which AI is superhuman in the lab and the economy looks normal. That period is where the political response would be decided.

### 4. Does the physical world follow quickly?
- **Quickly:** Forethought (robot doubling times of a year, then months), AI 2027 (a million robots a month in 2028), Musk.
- **Slowly:** L Rudolf L (data-limited until the 2030s), LeCun, Marcus, Morgan Stanley (mass adoption after 2035). Evidence: self-driving took about two decades of development to reach 500,000 paid rides a week ([Narayanan and Kapoor](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology) [opened]; [InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/791245/waymo-rides-per-week-2026-doubled/) [opened]); the best-known humanoid program has no units doing useful work; 71% of Americans oppose a local data center; power and water prices are rising.
- **What follows:** whether "post-labor" means all labor or only desk work for the next decade. If robots lag by ten years, the 2026–2036 economy has abundant cognition and scarce hands, and Baumol-style reallocation toward physical and in-person work is the main labor story.

### 5. With partial automation, does growth explode or get held back by what is left?
- **Explodes:** Epoch's GATE (above 20% growth with 30% of tasks automated), Barnett (remote work alone doubles or multiplies output), Amodei (10–20%).
- **Held back:** Cowen (O-ring and cost disease; +0.5 points), Acemoglu (about +0.1), economists even under "rapid" AI (3.5%), Karpathy (no break in the curve).
- **The technical core:** how substitutable automated work is for the rest. If poorly substitutable, the unautomated remainder sets the growth rate and its share of spending rises.
- **Market read:** real rates near 3% and a correction in AI stocks say investors are pricing the "held back" side with an investment boom on top.
- **What follows:** whether there is enough new output to pay for generous redistribution early (AI 2027's "stratospheric" tax revenue) or a fiscal squeeze first (L Rudolf L's Social Security crisis).

### 6. Who ends up with the income and the power, and do institutions respond?
- **Concentration by default:** Intelligence Curse, Gradual Disempowerment, Trammell and Patel, L Rudolf L, Varoufakis. Once labor is not needed, neither wages nor political leverage are guaranteed.
- **Institutions adapt:** Autor, Susskind's "Big State", Noah Smith (with limits on AI's resource use), Altman's "new social contract", the Fed governors who point to tax and competition policy.
- **Evidence so far:** adjustment through entry-level hiring; two private labs worth about $1T each; economists expecting a higher top-10% wealth share; but also an election in which AI build-out was an issue, widespread local opposition, and a public that favors UBI and job guarantees while most economists do not.
- **A sub-crux on the macro sign:** does displacement cause a demand collapse (Citrini) or does the build-out cause overheating (2026 so far)? This decides whether the first policy fight is over stimulus and income support or over inflation, power prices and data-center limits.
- **A second sub-crux on finance:** if the capex cycle breaks before the technology pays, as Zitron and Covello expect, progress slows for financial reasons and Barr's "stall" scenario applies for a few years regardless of cruxes 1–5.
