## 1. Industrial Revolution Britain

### 1.1 Engels' pause

Sources:
- Allen, R. C. (2009), "Engels' pause: Technical change, capital accumulation, and inequality in the British industrial revolution", *Explorations in Economic History* 46(4). https://www.nuffield.ox.ac.uk/Users/Allen/engelspause.pdf **[opened]**

| 1840–1900 | +90% | +123% |

Distribution (Allen 2009) **[opened]**:
- Labour's share of national income (real-price series): about 60% in the late 1700s, about 50% by mid-1800s, back to about 60% around 1900.
- Labour's share (nominal series): 50% in 1801, 45% in 1841.
- Capital's share: about 20% in the late 1700s, above 40% by mid-1800s.
- Gross profit rate: near 10% in the late 1700s, about 15% in the early 1800s, above 20% by mid-century.

**How long, and why.** On Allen's numbers real wages were flat for about 30 years (1800–1830), lagged for another 30 (1830–1860), and then grew faster than productivity, closing the gap by about 1900. So the pause was roughly one working lifetime and the full catch-up took about a century from the first acceleration.

Allen's explanation **[opened]**: new technology raised the demand for capital. Workers did not save, so investment had to come out of profits. Income therefore had to shift towards property owners before enough capital could be built. Once accumulation caught up (around mid-century), capital and effective labour grew at the same rate and wages rose with productivity. He treats Corn Law repeal (1846), emigration and cheap American grain as helpful but secondary.

**Where historians disagree.** Gregory Clark argues real wages rose faster and GDP slower than the Feinstein/Crafts–Harley figures, making workers the main gainers. Allen replies that even Clark's own wage index shows a pause, only a weaker one (Allen 2009, Appendix B) **[opened]**. An older "accidents" school blames war, harvests and the Corn Laws rather than technology (Allen 2009, section 2) **[opened]**.

**Why it matters for the guide.** The catch-up mechanism was capital accumulation raising the demand for labour, because capital and labour were complements. That is exactly the link full automation cuts.

### 1.2 The handloom weavers

Sources:
- Allen, R. C. (2017 working paper; published 2018 in *European Review of Economic History* 22(4)), "The hand-loom weaver and the power loom: a Schumpeterian perspective". https://nyuad.nyu.edu/content/dam/nyuad/academics/divisions/social-science/working-papers/2017/0004.pdf **[opened]**

Diffusion of the power loom in British cotton (Allen, Table 1) **[opened]**:

| Year | Handloom weavers | Power looms | Share of cloth woven by power |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1780 | 37,000 | – | – |
| 1792 | 95,000 | – | – |
| 1806 | 184,000 | hundreds | 0% |
| 1812 | 208,000 | 2,400 | 1% |
| 1820 | 240,000 | 14,150 | 10% |
| 1832 | 227,000 | 100,000 | 55% |
| 1835 | 188,000 | 108,894 | 71% |
| 1845 | 60,000 | 225,000 | 95% |
| 1850 | 43,000 | 249,627 | 97% |
| 1860 | 10,000 | 400,000 | 100% |

What the table shows:
- Peak to near-extinction took 40 years (1820–1860). The steep part was one decade: employment fell 68% between 1835 and 1845.
- **Wages fell before jobs did.** Allen: wages collapsed in the 1820s, employment followed in the 1830s and 1840s **[opened]**. Weavers kept working at falling piece rates for a decade or more.
- In the "golden age" (1793–1817) weavers earned 93% more than Allen's counterfactual, and roughly double or triple a labourer's pay. By mid-century building labourers earned three to four times what hand weavers did **[opened]**.
- Allen says about one in ten British men wove cotton in the early 1800s **[opened]**.
- The technology took about 25 years to go from 10% to 95% of output (1820–1845) **[opened]**.

What became of them: the trade died mostly by attrition. Older weavers stayed at the loom on subsistence earnings; children went to the mills or other work. I did not open Bythell's *The Handloom Weavers* (1969), which is the standard account of this; treat the attrition claim as **[secondary]**.

Allen also notes an earlier, less remembered displacement: machine spinning ended hand spinning by women in cottages across England, removing a second household income and worsening rural poverty **[opened]**.

### 1.3 The Luddites and the state's response

Sources:
- UK National Archives, "The proclamation of Ned Ludd". https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/stories/the-proclamation-of-ned-ludd/ **[opened]**
- Wikipedia, "Luddite". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddite **[opened: Wikipedia]**

- Machine-breaking began in Nottinghamshire in 1811 (first incident 11 March 1811 at Arnold), spread in 1812 to the West Riding of Yorkshire and Lancashire **[opened]**.
- Parliament made machine-breaking a capital crime in 1812 (Destruction of Stocking Frames, etc. Act) **[opened]**.
- About 12,000 troops, mostly militia and yeomanry, were deployed; Hobsbawm noted this was more than Wellington took to Portugal in 1808 **[opened: Wikipedia]**.
- A Special Commission at York in January 1813 led to 17 hangings; others were transported **[opened: National Archives]**. Over 60 men were charged and 30 acquitted **[opened: Wikipedia]**.
- The movement had faded by 1816–1817 **[opened: Wikipedia]**.
- Interpretation: Hobsbawm read machine-breaking as a bargaining tactic by workers with no legal means of bargaining, not as hostility to technology as such **[opened: Wikipedia]**.

The lag from first unrest to repression was under a year. The lag to any reform that helped the displaced was decades.

### 1.4 The Poor Laws and Speenhamland

Sources:

- Speenhamland: on 6 May 1795 Berkshire magistrates adopted a scale guaranteeing a household head, employed or not, a minimum weekly income tied to bread prices and family size. Such scales were mostly used in high-price years (1795–96, 1800–01) **[opened]**.

| 1783–85 | 2,004 | about 1.8–2.0% |
| 1803 | 4,268 | about 1.9–2.2% |
| 1813 | 6,656 | about 2.6% |
| 1818 | 7,871 (nominal peak) | – |
| 1821 | 6,959 | about 2.7% |
| 1831 | 6,799 | about 2.0% |
| 1841 | 4,761 | about 1.1% |
| 1851 | 4,963 | about 1.1% |

- Spending peaked at about 2.7% of GDP in 1818–20 **[opened]**.
- In 1803 about 1.04 million people, 11.4% of the population, received relief (official count) **[opened]**.
- The 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act grouped parishes into unions, created a central commission, and aimed to end outdoor relief for the able-bodied. Real per-capita relief spending fell 43% between 1831 and 1841 **[opened]**.

So the British state's response to the hardest decades of the transition was first to pay a wage supplement worth 2–3% of GDP, then to cut it sharply just as the handloom collapse reached its steepest phase.

**Where historians disagree.** The 1834 Royal Commission blamed allowances for idleness and low wages. Boyer cites Tawney's verdict that the report was influential but unhistorical. Blaug (1963) and Boyer (1990) explain regional relief patterns by economic conditions, and Boyer argues labour-hiring farmers used relief to shift slack-season labour costs onto other ratepayers **[opened]**. Historians also disagree on how far outdoor relief was really abolished after 1834 **[opened]**.

Peaks **[opened]**:
- Horses alone: 21.43 million, 1915.
- Mules alone: 5.92 million, 1925.
- Horses and mules together: 26.72 million, 1918 (Olmstead & Rhode give the same figure, 26.7 million in 1918).
- Decline from the 1918 peak to 1960: 88% in 42 years (my arithmetic). Down 46% by 1940; half gone by 1943 (25 years).

These are farm animals only. Off-farm horses (cities, mines) fell much earlier: about 3.45 million in 1910, 2.13 million in 1920, 380,000 in 1925 (Olmstead & Rhode, note 16) **[opened]**. Published "peak" dates differ (1910, 1915, 1918, 1920) because sources mix farm and non-farm animals, horses with or without mules, and census versus USDA dates.

### 2.2 Prices

- Nominal value per farm horse peaked around $111 in 1911, was $96 in 1920, $64 in 1925, and $53 in 1932 **[opened: Census]**. Consumer prices roughly doubled between 1913 and 1920, so the real fall was much steeper than the nominal one **[secondary]**.
- Olmstead & Rhode: the real value of a mature horse in 1925 was less than half its 1915 value **[opened]**.
- The price fall slowed the transition. Cheaper horses and cheaper feed (tractors released cropland, which lowered crop prices) kept the horse competitive for years. Olmstead & Rhode stress that the tractor made its rival cheaper **[opened]**. Draft animals had been eating the output of about 22% of harvested cropland; land used to feed them fell from 93 million acres (1915) to 65 million (1930) to 5 million (1960) **[opened]**.
- The late-1950s price recovery reflects a small remaining herd shifting to recreational use **[secondary]**.

### 2.3 What physically happened to the horses

Olmstead & Rhode **[opened]**:
- **Mostly they were not replaced.** Breeding collapsed in the 1920s. By 1931 the USDA reported breeders producing only about half of replacement needs. The average age of draft animals rose from about 9 years (1923) to over 12.5 (1935).
- **Slaughter was a minority route at first.** In 1940 only 19,000 of the 314,000 horses and mules that disappeared from farms (6%) went through federally inspected meat plants. The share rose in the late 1940s and early 1950s but stayed below half. Horsemeat went chiefly to pet food.
- Many farmers kept old animals after buying a tractor.
- The horse never vanished. The 1999 USDA equine inventory was 5.3 million, nearly all kept for recreation.

### 2.4 Britain

- Peak horse population about 3.3 million, late Victorian/Edwardian; decline from the 1900s; down about four-fifths by the 1970s (a historical-geography blog post, https://paddlefish-bear-csxj.squarespace.com/the-blog/historical-geographies-of-the-horse-in-britain) **[opened; non-scholarly]**. The usual scholarly source is F. M. L. Thompson, "Nineteenth-century horse sense", *Economic History Review* 29 (1976) **[secondary; not opened]**.
- Board of Trade horse census, Great Britain: 1,892,205 in 1924, which was 189,252 fewer than in 1920 (so about 2.08 million in 1920) **[opened: Hansard]**.
- London General Omnibus Company: 16,714 horses in 1901, last horse bus about ten years later **[opened: same blog]**.
- I found no clean annual British series.

### 2.5 Leontief's use of the comparison

- 1979 (*Challenge*) **[opened]**: Leontief uses horses and tractors to show that average-productivity statistics mislead during a substitution. He then draws the limit of the analogy himself: people, unlike horses, can reason and can vote. He argues that the market will no longer deliver shorter hours and a fair spread of income on its own, and that income policy through the tax and Social Security systems will be needed.
- 1983 (National Academy of Engineering symposium, "National Perspective: The Definition of Problems and Opportunities", p. 3) **[opened: Wikiquote; original page images unreadable]**: he argued that the role of humans as the most important factor of production would diminish in the way the role of horses in farming was first reduced and then eliminated by tractors.

### 3.4 Where the workers went, and how long it took

- Into manufacturing, construction, trade and services, largely by moving to cities, and largely between generations. The big outflow came 1940–1970, not when the tractor arrived in the 1920s **[opened: series above]**.
- Many farm households adjusted in place. About 30% of farm operators worked off-farm at least 100 days in 1930; by 1970 over half of farm households had off-farm income; by 2002, 93% **[opened: USDA ERS]**.
- Timescale: from 40% of the labour force (1900) to 8% (1960) took 60 years; the absolute fall was concentrated in about 30 years.

## 4. Trade and deindustrialisation

### 4.1 The China shock

Sources (NBER abstract pages unless noted):
- Autor, Dorn & Hanson (2013), "The China Syndrome". https://www.nber.org/papers/w18054 **[opened]**
- Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson & Price (2016), "Import Competition and the Great US Employment Sag of the 2000s". https://www.nber.org/papers/w20395 **[opened]**
- Autor, Dorn & Hanson (2021), "On the Persistence of the China Shock". https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401 **[opened]**; Brookings summary https://www.brookings.edu/articles/on-the-persistence-of-the-china-shock/ **[opened]**
- Autor, Dorn & Hanson (2019), "When Work Disappears". https://www.nber.org/papers/w23173 **[opened]**
- Pierce & Schott (2020), "Trade Liberalization and Mortality". https://www.nber.org/papers/w22849 **[opened]**
- Autor, Dorn, Hanson & Majlesi (2020), "Importing Political Polarization?". https://www.nber.org/papers/w22637 **[opened]**
- Wikipedia, "China shock". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_shock **[opened: Wikipedia]**

Findings:
- **Jobs.** Chinese import competition explains about a quarter of the fall in US manufacturing employment, 1990–2007 (ADH 2013). Net losses of 2.0–2.4 million jobs, 1999–2011, counting supplier and local-demand effects (Acemoglu et al.) **[opened]**.
- **Persistence.** The import surge levelled off around 2010. Local effects were still present in 2019. Over 2000–2019 exposure cut the manufacturing employment-to-population ratio by 1.54 percentage points (55% of the observed change), and 86% of that showed up as a lower overall employment rate, not as jobs elsewhere **[opened]**.
- **People did not move.** Population fell only among the foreign-born and native-born adults aged 25–39. Adjustment came mainly through leaving the labour force **[opened]**.
- **Income.** Exposed commuting zones saw income per head fall by more than trade models predict; a substantial share saw absolute falls in real income. Transfers rose only modestly, mostly Social Security and Medicare. Trade Adjustment Assistance had a negligible effect on incomes **[opened]**.
- **Family.** Shocks to manufacturing reduced young men's employment and earnings relative to women's, lowered marriage and birth rates, raised the share of unmarried mothers and of children in poor single-parent homes, and raised young men's early mortality **[opened]**.
- **Mortality.** Counties more exposed to trade liberalisation with China had higher suicide and related death rates, concentrated among white men **[opened]**.
- **Politics.** Exposed districts removed moderates in the 2000s; majority-white areas moved to conservative Republicans, majority-minority areas to liberal Democrats; exposed counties shifted Republican in presidential votes **[opened]**. The authors' counterfactual: with 50% less import growth, Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania would likely have gone Democratic in 2016 **[opened: Wikipedia]**.
- ADH themselves say the pattern resembles the decline of coal employment since the 1980s **[opened]**.

**Critics and counter-estimates** **[opened: Wikipedia]**: Caliendo, Dvorkin & Parro (2019) put the loss at about 550,000 manufacturing jobs. Feenstra & Sasahara (2018) find 1.8–2.0 million. Jaravel & Sager (2019) estimate consumer price gains worth about $411,000 per manufacturing job lost. ADH's own 2021 reassessment, after counting lower prices, finds about 6.3% of the US population were net losers.

The lesson is about ratio. A shock equal to roughly 1.5% of US employment, with net aggregate gains, was enough to leave measurable damage 20 years on and to move national elections.

### 4.2 Deaths of despair

Sources:
- Case & Deaton (2017), "Mortality and morbidity in the 21st century", Brookings. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/mortality-and-morbidity-in-the-21st-century/ **[opened]**
- Wikipedia, "Diseases of despair". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diseases_of_despair **[opened: Wikipedia]**
- Case & Deaton (2015), *PNAS* 112(49). https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1518393112 **[secondary; returned 403]**

- Mortality among middle-aged white non-Hispanic Americans with a high-school education or less has risen since the late 1990s, while midlife mortality fell in other rich countries. That group's mortality was about 30% below Black Americans' in 1999 and about 30% above by 2015 **[opened: Brookings]**.
- US deaths from drugs, alcohol and suicide: about 65,000 in 1995, about 158,000 in 2018 **[opened: Wikipedia]**.
- 2015 paper: all-cause mortality for whites aged 45–54 rose by about half a percent a year from 1999 to 2013 after falling about 2% a year before; had the earlier decline continued, roughly half a million deaths would have been avoided **[secondary]**.
- Case & Deaton's explanation is cumulative: worsening job prospects for the less educated since the 1970s, then falling marriage, participation and health cohort by cohort **[opened: Brookings]**.
- **Counter-view.** Critics argue the opioid supply (OxyContin, then fentanyl) explains more than economic despair, and note that European income shocks did not produce similar mortality **[opened: Wikipedia]**.

### 4.3 Displaced workers' earnings and health

Sources:
- Jacobson, LaLonde & Sullivan (1993), "Earnings Losses of Displaced Workers", *AER* 83(4). https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v83y1993i4p685-709.html **[opened: abstract]**
- Davis & von Wachter (2011), "Recessions and the Costs of Job Loss". https://www.nber.org/papers/w17638 **[opened: abstract]**
- Sullivan & von Wachter (2009), "Job Displacement and Mortality", *QJE* 124(3). https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v124y2009i3p1265-1306..html **[opened: abstract]**

- Long-tenured workers who lose jobs at distressed firms suffer lasting earnings losses averaging 25% a year. Losses start before separation and are large even for those re-employed in similar firms (JLS) **[opened]**. The data are Pennsylvania administrative records from the 1970s–80s **[secondary]**.
- Men displaced in mass layoffs lose about 1.4 years of pre-displacement earnings in present value when unemployment is below 6%, and 2.8 years when it is above 8%, over 20 years at a 5% discount rate (Davis & von Wachter) **[opened]**.
- Death rates for displaced long-tenured men are 50–100% higher in the year after job loss and still 10–15% higher 20 years later, implying 1.0–1.5 years of lost life for a worker displaced at 40 (Sullivan & von Wachter) **[opened]**.

### 5.2 Size and mechanism of the collapse

- August 1929 to March 1933: industrial production −52.6%; real GNP about −29% (1929–1933); prices about −33%; money supply about −35%. By March 1933 more than a third of US banks had closed or been absorbed **[opened: EH.net]**.
- **Debt deflation (Fisher).** By March 1933 liquidation had cut nominal debts by about 20%, but the dollar's purchasing power had risen about 75%, so the real debt burden had *risen* about 40%. The harder debtors tried to pay, the more they owed in real terms, and the spiral had no natural stopping point **[opened]**.
- **Monetary contraction (Friedman & Schwartz).** Bank failures shrank the money stock and the Federal Reserve did not offset it **[opened: EH.net]**.
- **Credit channel (Bernanke).** Bank losses destroyed lending relationships; households, farmers and small firms lost access to credit **[opened: EH.net]**.
- **Gold standard (Eichengreen, Temin).** Commitment to gold forced deflation; countries recovered in the order they left gold **[opened: EH.net]**.
- Recovery: trough March 1933; real GNP regained its 1929 level in 1937 and its pre-Depression trend in 1942. EH.net credits the war, not New Deal fiscal policy, with ending it **[opened]**.

### 5.4 Movements

- **Technocracy.** Howard Scott's group proposed replacing money with non-transferable energy certificates issued equally to every citizen. Its heyday ran from June 1932 to January 1933 and ended after a badly received radio address. Historians disagree on why it faded: the New Deal's arrival, or its lack of any political strategy (Akin) **[opened: Wikipedia]**.
- **Townsend Plan.** Proposed September 1933: $200 a month for everyone over 60, to be spent within the month, funded by a 2% transactions tax. By early 1936 about 2 million members in about 8,000 clubs; the organisation claimed 20 million petition signatures. Its bill lost in the House 56–206. Frances Perkins recorded Roosevelt saying Congress could not withstand Townsend pressure without a real old-age insurance system **[opened: Wikipedia]**.
- **Share Our Wealth.** Huey Long, February 1934: caps on fortunes, incomes and inheritances; a $5,000 household estate; a guaranteed family income of $2,000–3,000; pensions. By 1935 the society claimed over 7.5 million members in 27,000 clubs. Roosevelt's 1935 "Second New Deal" (Social Security, the Wealth Tax Act) is often read as an attempt to take Long's ground. Long was shot on 8 September 1935 **[opened: Wikipedia]**.

Pattern: the programmes that passed were moderate versions of demands made by mass movements that had formed within four to five years of the crash.

## 6. Earlier automation scares

Sources:
- Keynes, J. M. (1930), "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren". https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/keynes/1930/our-grandchildren.htm **[opened]**
- Ad Hoc Committee (1964), "The Triple Revolution". Text: https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/isr/vol25/no03/adhoc.html **[opened]**; Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Triple_Revolution **[opened: Wikipedia]**
- National Commission on Technology, Automation, and Economic Progress (1966), *Technology and the American Economy*, vol. 1. https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED023803.pdf **[opened]**
- Wikipedia, "Family Assistance Plan". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_Assistance_Plan **[opened: Wikipedia]**
- Wikipedia, "Technological unemployment". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_unemployment **[opened: Wikipedia]**

### 6.1 The 1930s

- Keynes (1930) defined technological unemployment as labour-saving outrunning the discovery of new uses for labour, called it a temporary phase of maladjustment, forecast living standards four to eight times higher in a hundred years, and suggested three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week to spread the remaining work **[opened]**.
- US debate: urban unemployment began rising in 1927 and economists returned to the question; the debate peaked in the 1930s; it faded when the war removed unemployment. A major federal study near the end found no long-run technological unemployment but did find short-run displacement and recommended help for the displaced **[opened: Wikipedia]**.
- Scorecard on Keynes: the income forecast is roughly on track for rich countries; the hours forecast was far off **[secondary]**.

### 6.2 The 1960s

- **The Triple Revolution**, sent to President Johnson on 22 March 1964 by about 35 signatories (Pauling, Myrdal, Heilbroner, Harrington, Rustin, Hayden among them). It argued that "cybernation" was breaking the link between jobs and income, and called for an unconditional right to an adequate income. It cited unemployment at or above 5.5%, an estimated 8 million people wanting work against 4 million officially unemployed, and productivity growth above 3.5% a year in 1961–63 **[opened]**.
- **National Commission** (Public Law 88-444, 1964; chair Howard R. Bowen; members included Daniel Bell, Thomas J. Watson Jr. and Walter Reuther; report February 1966) **[opened]**. Its central finding, in its own words: "technology eliminates jobs, not work." The level of demand, not technology, decides how many are unemployed. Yet it recommended:
  - public service employment, with government as employer of last resort for the hard-core unemployed;
  - a floor under family income, and serious study of a minimum income allowance or negative income tax;
  - 14 years of free public education.
- The scare ended as unemployment fell in the Vietnam War boom **[opened: Wikipedia]**. Later judgments differ: Daniel Bell called the cybernation revolution illusory; Martin Ford argues the forecasts were early, not wrong **[opened: Wikipedia]**.

### 6.3 Nixon's Family Assistance Plan

All **[opened: Wikipedia]** unless marked.
- Announced August 1969. A family of four with no other income would get $1,600 a year, with a 50% reduction rate on earnings and a work-registration requirement.
- It would have newly covered about 13 million people at a projected cost of $4.4 billion.
- The House passed it in April 1970 (243–155 **[secondary]**) and again inside H.R. 1 in June 1971 (288–132 **[secondary]**).
- It died twice in the Senate Finance Committee (1970, 1972). On 3 October 1972 a guaranteed-income amendment (Ribicoff) was tabled 52–24. H.R. 1 passed without it.
- It was killed from both sides: conservatives and Southern Democrats objected to cost and work disincentives; the National Welfare Rights Organization wanted $5,500 with no work requirement and called the plan too stingy.
- What survived was narrower: Supplemental Security Income for the aged, blind and disabled (1972) and the Earned Income Tax Credit (1975) **[secondary]**.

Timeline: idea in a public memo (1964), endorsed for study by a federal commission (1966), proposed by a president (1969), passed one chamber twice (1970, 1971), dead (1972). Eight years, and the closest the US has come.

## 7. Rentier and resource states

### 7.1 The resource-curse literature

Sources:
- Wikipedia, "Resource curse" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_curse ; "Rentier state" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rentier_state **[opened: Wikipedia]**
- Sala-i-Martin & Subramanian (2003), "Addressing the Natural Resource Curse: An Illustration from Nigeria". https://www.nber.org/papers/w9804 **[opened: abstract]**

- **Rentier state** (Mahdavy 1970; Beblawi & Luciani 1987): the state lives on external rent; few people are involved in producing it; the government receives it and distributes it; because it does not need to tax, it faces less pressure to be accountable **[opened]**.
- **Mechanisms claimed:** Dutch disease; revenue volatility; no taxation, so no representation; rents used to buy loyalty and fund security forces; enclave production with few jobs; conflict over control **[opened]**.
- **Ross.** His 2015 review concludes that only petroleum is consistently linked to less democracy and worse institutions **[opened: Wikipedia quoting Ross]**.
- **Critics.** Haber & Menaldo (2011) find, within countries over time, that rising resource reliance does not cause authoritarianism. Andersen & Ross reply that this holds only before the 1970s; since about 1980, when states captured the rents from foreign firms, the curse appears. A 2016 meta-study (Havranek et al.) found about 40% of papers reporting a negative growth effect, 40% none, 20% positive. Dunning argues rents can ease redistribution by lowering elites' fear of it **[opened]**.

The timing point matters for the guide: the "curse" shows up once the **state**, not private foreign owners, holds the rent.

### 7.3 Norway, Nigeria, Venezuela

- **Norway.** The Government Pension Fund Global was set up in 1990. Value about NOK 22.7 trillion in mid-2026, roughly US$2.2–2.5 trillion, over US$390,000 per citizen, and about 1.5% of the world's listed equity. A fiscal rule limits withdrawals to 3% of the fund per year (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Pension_Fund_of_Norway) **[opened: Wikipedia; check against Norges Bank Investment Management]**.

Their paper's figures: roughly $350 billion of oil revenue over 35 years, income per head no higher in 2000 than in 1970, and the share living on under a dollar a day rising from about 36% to about 70% **[secondary; from the paper body, not opened]**.
- **Venezuela.** Real GDP fell an estimated 24.3% in 2014–2016 alone; inflation reached 800% in 2016; surveys found about 94% in income poverty in 2019–2021; 7.7 million people had emigrated by 2024 (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_in_Venezuela) **[opened: Wikipedia]**. The total GDP fall from 2013 to 2021 is usually put at about three-quarters **[secondary]**.

Same resource, opposite outcomes. What differed was the quality of institutions **before** the windfall and whether the rent was saved under a rule or spent through patronage.

### 7.5 Nauru

Sources: Wikipedia, "Nauru" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nauru and "Economy of Nauru" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Nauru **[opened: Wikipedia]**

- Phosphate rent made Nauru one of the richest countries per head in the 1970s; GDP peaked in 1981 **[opened; the "richest in the world" claim is flagged as unsourced on the page]**.
- About 80% of the island's surface was strip-mined; primary deposits were essentially gone by 2000 **[opened]**.
- The national trust fell from A$1.3 billion (1991) to A$138 million (2002) after failed investments, including a London musical; hotels and office towers were sold to pay debts; the national bank failed in 2006 **[opened]**.

Nauru is the worst-case rentier outcome: a finite rent, spent, with no productive base or habits left behind.

### 8.1 The grain dole

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 123 BC | Gaius Gracchus's grain law: eligible adult male citizens may buy 5 modii (about 33 kg) a month below market price; about 40,000 initially eligible |
| 58 BC | Clodius makes it free; an estimated 320,000 recipients |
| c. 46 BC (date **[secondary]**) | Caesar cuts the list to 150,000 |
| Augustus | List set at about 200,000, roughly stable for centuries |
| AD 193–211 | Septimius Severus adds olive oil (and probably bread instead of grain) |

| early 7th c. | Ended in Constantinople when Egypt was lost to Persia |

All **[opened: Wikipedia]**.

- A ration of 5 modii was ample for two people but below subsistence for three **[opened]**. It was a supplement for citizen households in one city, not an income.
- Rome imported an estimated 150,000–270,000 tonnes of grain a year; most was traded commercially, not distributed by the state **[opened]**.
- Cost: I found no reliable figure. Modern estimates in the literature are of the order of a few percent of the imperial budget **[secondary; unverified]**.
- Duration: roughly 500–600 years in Rome. Once granted it was never withdrawn by choice, only cut back in scope (Caesar) and ended by state collapse.

### 8.2 The traditional story and its revision

- **Traditional story** (Plutarch, Appian; Pliny's remark that large estates ruined Italy): after the Punic Wars the rich took over public land and worked it with war-captive slaves; free smallholders, away on campaign, were displaced, drifted to Rome and became a dependent urban mass that the dole fed **[opened: Wikipedia]**.
- **What scholars now say** **[opened: Wikipedia, citing Roselaar 2010, de Ligt 2006, Lintott 1994]**:
  - Field-survey archaeology since the 1980s finds small farms across Italy in the second century BC. The decline of the countryside has been much overstated.
  - Archaeology does not support slave estates spreading over public land at that time.
  - The fall in the citizen census (328,316 to 317,933 between 159 and 136 BC) may reflect draft evasion, and the sharp rises soon after are inconsistent with real depopulation.
  - The better explanation for rural distress is **too many people, not too few**: population growth and division of farms among heirs produced plots too small to live on, which were then sold to the rich.
  - The slave-estate picture probably generalised from particular areas such as coastal Etruria.
  - Even later, free tenants persisted alongside slaves on large estates; Pliny's claim that six men owned half of Africa is probably rhetoric.
- Slave numbers: older estimates put slaves at 2–3 million of about 6–7.5 million people in Augustan Italy (Brunt); Scheidel's lower estimates are 1–1.5 million **[secondary; not opened]**. Rosenstein's *Rome at War* (2004) argues that military service was compatible with small farming **[secondary; not opened]**.

Use with care. Rome is weak evidence for "automation-like displacement leads to bread and circuses". It is better evidence for two narrower points: an entitlement, once created, can last for centuries; and it was restricted to a privileged citizen body.

### 9.3 Reading

- When income is concentrated and human labour is cheap relative to the incomes of the rich, a large share of workers end up selling personal attendance to the rich. On the unverified British figures above, Britain, the more unequal society, had a servant share roughly double the American one (the British category is also somewhat broader).
- Service shrank when alternatives improved and wages rose (factory and office work, immigration restriction, then appliances), not because the rich stopped wanting servants. Stigler's 1946 study links servant numbers to income inequality and to the supply of immigrant and Black women with few alternatives **[secondary; not opened]**.
- For the guide: this is the historical shape of "jobs created by rich people's spending". It is real and can be large (5–15% of the workforce), it is low-paid and deferential, and under full automation it survives only where a human is wanted **because** they are human.

## 10. Why elites share

Sources:

### 10.1 The case that leverage drove sharing

- **Mass warfare and taxing the rich (Scheve & Stasavage).** Across 20 countries and two centuries, steeply progressive taxation arrived with mass mobilisation for war, not with democracy or with inequality as such. The winning argument was compensatory: if ordinary people are conscripted, wealth must be conscripted too. Comparing First World War participants with non-participants, mobilisation is followed by a large rise in tax progressivity; universal suffrage and left governments alone are not **[opened]**. They add that because modern war no longer needs mass armies, that argument has lost force **[opened]**.
- **US top marginal income tax rate** **[opened: Tax Foundation]** except where marked:

| Year | Top rate |
|---|---|
| 1913 | 7% **[secondary]** |
| 1918 | 77% **[secondary]** |
| 1925 | 25% |
| 1932 | 63% |
| 1936 | 79% |

| 1942 | 88% |
| 1944–45 | 94% |
| 1952 | 92% |
| 1964 | 77% |
| 1981 | 70% |

- **Military participation ratio (Andreski, 1954).** The larger the share of the population a state needs under arms, the flatter its social pyramid tends to be **[secondary; not opened]**. Titmuss (1958) made the parallel argument for the British welfare state **[secondary]**.
- **Threat of revolution (Acemoglu & Robinson).** Nineteenth-century elites extended the vote to head off unrest. A promise of redistribution is not believable while elites keep power; handing over the vote is a commitment that is. Democratisation then produced the redistribution that bent inequality down **[opened]**.
- **Evidence for the threat mechanism (Aidt & Franck).** In the 1831 election, English constituencies near Swing riots (measured within 10 km, across 244 constituencies and about 344,000 voters) were more likely to elect pro-reform MPs, which helped pass the 1832 Reform Act **[opened]**.
- **Violence as the only reliable leveller (Scheidel, *The Great Leveler*, 2017).** Large falls in inequality have come from mass-mobilisation war, transformative revolution, state collapse and lethal pandemics; peaceful reform has rarely achieved comparable compression **[secondary; publisher page returned 403]**. His pandemic case is the Black Death, where labour scarcity raised real wages: workers gained because they became scarce.

Common thread: ordinary people got a share when elites **needed** them as workers, soldiers or taxpayers, or **feared** them.

### 10.2 Counter-views

- **Sharing can be in the elite's own interest (Lizzeri & Persico).** A majority of the British elite may have favoured wider suffrage with no threat at all, because a broad electorate pushes politicians from private favours to public goods (sanitation, public health) that the elite also wanted **[opened]**.
- **Peacetime welfare states.** Bismarck's social insurance (1880s) came in peacetime as a move against socialists; neutral Sweden and Switzerland built large welfare states; much of the growth in social spending came in the peaceful decades after 1950 (Lindert's *Growing Public* stresses voice and ageing electorates) **[secondary]**.
- **Ideas and institutions.** Piketty attributes twentieth-century compression to political choices and ideology as well as shocks **[secondary]**.
- **Regressive war finance.** Many mass wars were paid for by inflation and debt, which fall on ordinary savers **[secondary]**.
- **Rome and the Gulf** show sharing with a privileged citizen body without democracy, when rulers want quiet.

### 10.3 Why it matters

Full automation removes the first two levers (labour and soldiers). What remains is the vote, where it already exists, and the capacity to disrupt. History has no clean case of a population keeping a large share of income for long once it was needed for neither production nor defence. The nearest cases are rentier states, where the share depends on citizenship rules and the ruler's wish for stability.

## 12. Verdict

### 12.1 Which analogies apply, and where each breaks

| Analogy | What transfers | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| **Horses** | The closest case of a labour input losing its value outright. Price fell first (real value halved in ten years); that slowed the switch; numbers then fell 88% over 42 years, mainly by not being replaced. A remnant survived as a luxury. | Horses were owned, did not vote, did not consume the product, and their owners controlled their numbers. Leontief drew this limit himself. |
| **Handloom weavers / Engels' pause** | Wages fall before jobs go. One trade can be wiped out in 25–40 years. Aggregate wages can stay flat for a generation while output and profits rise. Repression arrives faster than help. | Wages caught up because capital accumulation raised demand for labour. With full automation, accumulation no longer does that. |
| **US farm labour** | Share-of-workforce collapse on a 60-year S-curve; the actual exodus waits for a pull from elsewhere and reverses in a slump. | There was an "elsewhere". Full automation removes it. |
| **China shock, coal, steel** | People do not move; non-employment persists 10–40 years; marriage, health and politics are damaged; a 1–2% national shock can swing elections. | These were local shocks in a growing national economy that could have paid for adjustment. A general shock leaves no healthy remainder, but also no minority to blame. |
| **Great Depression** | The demand-collapse and debt-deflation mechanism if wage income falls and nothing replaces it. Mass, cross-class distress produces radical movements within 4–5 years and moderate legislation in response. | Output was falling then. Under automation output rises, so the means to pay exist; the problem is purely distribution. |
| **Rentier states** | The only real economies where citizens' income does not depend on their labour. Outcomes run from Norway to Nauru, decided by prior institutions and by whether rent is saved under rules. The common equilibrium is state employment as a disguised transfer, with real work done by a rightless underclass. | Resource rent is paid by foreigners who still work, and belongs to the state by default. Automation rent is domestic and belongs to private owners by default, so it must first be taxed or owned publicly. |
| **Alaska dividend** | A universal cash dividend is popular, does not reduce work at modest scale, and is hard to abolish. | It is small, and its size has been cut at will since 2016. Durable in existence, not in value. |
| **Rome** | Entitlements for a citizen body can last centuries. | The displacement story behind it is largely discredited; the dole was a food supplement for about 200,000 households in one city. |
| **Domestic service** | Shows the kind of work that concentrated wealth creates: personal attendance, 5–15% of the workforce. | Machines that can do all labour can do this too, except where humanness is the point. |
| **War mobilisation, CARES** | States can move in weeks and remake an economy in four years when the threat is sudden, universal and visible. | Both were framed as temporary, and the 2020–21 gains were reversed within a year. |

### 12.2 Base rates

Adjustment times:
- A superior production technology takes 25–45 years to go from about 5–10% to about 75–95% of its market (power loom 25, factory electricity about 30, tractor 39, cross-country average 45). Consumer access now takes under 5.
- The displaced factor's price falls within the first decade, well before quantities fall.
- A displaced occupation takes 25–40 years to disappear and does so mostly by attrition and non-entry, not by retraining of incumbents.
- A displaced worker loses about 25% of earnings for many years, one and a half to three years of earnings in present value, and one to one and a half years of life.
- A displaced region is still behind after 20 years (China shock) and after 40 (coalfields).
- Economy-wide wages lagged productivity for 30–60 years in the one fully documented case.

Political lags:

| Episode | Lag to first major response | Nature of response |
|---|---|---|
| Luddism, 1811 | under 1 year | Repression |
| British industrial distress, c. 1800–1840 | 30–70 years (1832, 1867, 1884 franchise; factory acts 1833–1847) | Relief cut in 1834; reform slow |
| Great Depression, 1929 | 3.4 years (banking, relief), 5.8 years (Social Security), 10 years (first pensions paid) | Structural, lasting |
| 1960s automation scare | 5 years to a presidential bill; failed at 8 | Narrow substitutes (SSI, EITC) |
| China shock, c. 2000 | no effective adjustment policy in 20 years (trade adjustment aid negligible) | Political realignment; tariffs from 2018 |
| Covid, 2020 | 14 days | Large, effective, temporary |
| War, 1940 | months; full conversion in 2–4 years | Total, temporary |

What separates fast from slow:
- **Fast** when the shock is sudden, visible, hits the middle of the income distribution, cannot be blamed on its victims, threatens owners and creditors too (through collapsing demand or defeat), and can be met through payment systems that already exist.
- **Slow or hostile** when the shock is gradual, concentrated in particular trades or places, and its victims are a minority who can be told to adapt.

Full automation would end as the first kind of shock but begin as the second, one occupation at a time. History suggests the dangerous stretch is the transition: the years when the displaced are numerous enough to suffer but not yet numerous enough to be the electorate.

One structural warning from section 10. Every historical mechanism that made elites share rested on needing people as workers or soldiers, or fearing them. The precedents for sharing without that need are rentier states, and their record is mixed.

## What cuts against the obvious story

1. **The horse transition was slow and mostly bloodless.** It took 40 years, falling horse prices delayed it, and most animals were simply not bred rather than destroyed. The image of sudden mass disposal is wrong. The horse population later tripled from its low as a leisure good.
2. **The Engels' pause is contested, and it ended without redistribution.** Clark thinks workers gained throughout. Even on Allen's account, wages caught up through accumulation, not through politics. Anyone citing the pause as proof that workers lose must also explain why they then won.
3. **The Old Poor Law was not the failure its abolishers claimed.** Britain ran a wage-supplement system costing 2–3% of GDP for 40 years, and the charge that it bred idleness is now largely rejected. The cautionary tale may run the other way: support was cut in 1834 just before the worst of the handloom collapse.
4. **Farm workers were not thrown off the land by the tractor in the 1920s.** Farm employment and population held steady for 30 years after tractors arrived, rose in the Depression, and fell only when wartime and post-war cities pulled people in. Pull mattered more than push.
5. **The China shock was small in aggregate and positive on net.** Estimates of job losses range from 0.55 to 2.4 million in an economy of 150 million jobs, with consumer gains that exceeded the losses for about 94% of people. The damage story is about concentration and non-adjustment, not size.
6. **Deaths of despair may be mainly a drug-supply story.** Comparable income shocks in Europe did not raise mortality.
7. **The Depression was a monetary and financial collapse, not a technological one,** and both the 1930s and 1960s automation scares were wrong about aggregate employment. A federal commission of serious people concluded in 1966 that demand, not technology, sets the level of employment, and for the next 60 years it was right.
8. **The guaranteed income nearly passed under a Republican president in 1970,** and was killed partly by advocates for the poor who wanted more. The obstacle was not only the right.
9. **The resource curse is not a law.** Within-country evidence is mixed, the meta-analysis is split 40/40/20, and Norway, Alaska and (on the page I did not reach) Botswana manage rents well. Alaska's dividend has no measurable effect on employment.
10. **The Roman story that slave estates created a workless mob fed by the dole is largely discredited.** Italy's rural problem was probably overpopulation, and the dole reached a minority of one city.
11. **Elites have sometimes shared without being forced.** Lizzeri & Persico's reading of Victorian Britain, Bismarck's peacetime insurance, and neutral Sweden all show welfare states arising without mass war. And unlike 1832, universal suffrage already exists.
12. **Speed cuts both ways.** Half of US adults adopted generative AI in three years, far faster than the PC or internet, yet it touched under 6% of work hours. Every earlier production technology took decades to restructure work. Against that, states have shown they can act in two weeks and re-tool an economy in four years.
13. **Fast policy has been temporary policy.** The 2020–21 income support was the largest and quickest in US history and was allowed to lapse within two years, with child poverty more than doubling. Speed of response and durability of response are separate questions.
